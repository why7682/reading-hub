    Multi-Objective Optimization: A Survey Across Five Communities#

    Advanced graduate-level survey. Audience: researchers entering MOO from one community (operations research, evolutionary computation, deep multi-task learning, multi-objective reinforcement learning, or LLM alignment) who need to read across the others without re-deriving vocabulary.

    How to read this survey#

    What this is. A cross-community map of multi-objective optimization. Five fields — OR / MCDM, evolutionary MOO, deep multi-task learning, multi-objective reinforcement learning, and LLM alignment — re-derive the same Pareto-optimality concepts under different names. This survey supplies the translation table (§3), the order-theoretic core that grounds it (§2), the per-community apparatus (§4–§7), the lateral threads that connect them (§8), and a critical-reading toolkit (§9). The target reader is an advanced graduate student or researcher in one of the five communities who wants to read the others without re-learning the vocabulary.

    Reading paths by community. - OR / MCDM: read §2 (which formalises what you already know), then §5 (deep-MTL re-derivations of scalarisation), §7 (RLHF's social-choice corner). - Deep MTL: read §2 (the order-theoretic core MTL papers usually skip), §5 (the gradient revolution as MOO), §9 (critical reading on \(\Delta m\%\) and the gradient-surgery \(k\ge 3\) gap). - MORL: read §2, §6, then §7 (Safe RLHF as CMDP). - Alignment / RLHF: read §3 (vocabulary), §7 (full RLHF-as-MOO development), §8 (lateral threads: welfare, \(f\)-divergence, Goodhart). - Everyone reads §2 first; §10–§11 are the open-problems list and reading list.

    Prerequisites. Single-objective convex optimisation through KKT; linear algebra including cone-induced partial orders; basic neural-network MTL and policy-gradient RL; light familiarity with social-choice theory (Arrow's theorem) and information theory (\(f\)-divergence) is helpful but not assumed. Tropical geometry (§4.7), DRO (§7.12), and Csiszár information geometry (§8.6) are flagged as advanced and can be skipped at first pass.

    A note on caveats. Throughout we use the marker [†caveat-X] to flag claims that are surveyor synthesis rather than direct literature citation, or that rest on assumptions worth flagging. Examples: [†caveat-C] for the "common descent cone" framing of §5 (pedagogical synthesis, not a phrase in the literature); [†caveat-T] for the Tchebycheff-tropical analogy (informal, not yet a published theorem); [†caveat-Lucas] for the Goodhart-vs-Lucas distinction. The list is summarised in §9.5.

    Loosening lines, not a single thread. A reader familiar with the bilevel optimization literature will recognise the rhetorical move of organising a survey around a single technical assumption whose successive relaxation drives the field forward (e.g., bilevel's lower-level-singleton assumption). MOO does not work this way. The field has three or four parallel loosening lines — the convex-front assumption, the static-weight assumption, the single-solution-output assumption — and they belong to different communities. We use the phrase "loosening thread" to name this structure; §1 develops it.

    §0 Notation#

    We adopt the notation of Ehrgott (2005) and Désidéri (2014) throughout; per-symbol justification is given in Table 1. Three conventions are essential to the survey's correctness and depart from common informal usage.

    First, the unit simplex with \(m\) vertices is an \((m-1)\)-dimensional affine object — the constraint \(\sum_i w_i = 1\) removes one degree of freedom — so its standard name is \(\Delta^{m-1}\), not \(\Delta^m\). Equation (E1): $\(\Delta^{m-1} = \Big\{\,w \in \mathbb{R}^m_{\ge 0}\ :\ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^m w_i = 1\,\Big\}. \tag{E1}\)$ This convention is uniform in Ehrgott (2005) and Miettinen (1999); the variant "\(\Delta^m\)" for the \(m\)-vertex case is off by one [†caveat-M].

    Second, we distinguish two simplex-valued objects that are routinely conflated in the MTL literature. We write \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \Delta^{k-1}\) for computed task weights — the output of an algorithm such as MGDA's quadratic program, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\star = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}}\|\sum_i \alpha_i \nabla J_i\|^2\). We write \(\boldsymbol{\omega} \in \Delta^{k-1}\) for user preference weights — an input to a method such as preference-conditioned hypernetworks (PHN, Navon et al. 2021) or Rewarded Soups (Rame et al. 2023). The two never appear in the same role, and informal MTL writing routinely conflates them; the discipline here is to keep the symbols separate. Where Désidéri's min-norm element would clash with the preference symbol, we use \(\mathbf{d}^\star\) in body prose.

    Third, three reference points appear in distinct roles. The utopia (or ideal) point \(z^\star\in\mathbb{R}^k\) is the componentwise minimum \(z_i^\star = \min_{x\in S} f_i(x)\) and serves as the Tchebycheff anchor. The reference point \(z^{\mathrm{ref}}\) in Wierzbicki's achievement scalarization is decision-maker-supplied and need not be feasible. The hypervolume reference \(r\in\mathbb{R}^k\) is the dominator \(r\succeq A\) used to bound the dominated region for HV computation; it is distinct from \(z^\star\) and from \(z^{\mathrm{ref}}\).

    §1 The Dilemma and Three Threads#

    MOO does not have a single loosening thread; it has three or four parallel ones, each driven by a different community. To see why this matters, consider how the field's foundational worry has been phrased across forty years. Ehrgott's textbook opens with the observation that the decision maker faces "conflicting, often incommensurable, objectives to be simultaneously evaluated and optimized" (Ehrgott 2005, blurb). Twenty-three years earlier, Sawaragi, Nakayama, and Tanino had already formalised the same intuition through the partial order induced by a domination cone, and Hwang and Masud (1979) had organised the surrounding methodological landscape by when the decision maker articulates a preference. By 2018 the dilemma reappears, almost word for word, in Sener and Koltun's reframing of multi-task learning: "Multi-task learning is inherently a multi-objective problem because different tasks may conflict, necessitating a trade-off" (Sener and Koltun 2018, abstract). By 2024 the LLM alignment community states the same thing again — Panacea (Zhong et al. 2024) writes that scalar preference labels "oversimplify the multi-dimensional and heterogeneous nature of human preferences, leading to reduced expressivity and even misalignment."

    The recurrence is itself remarkable. What is harder to explain is that each community has independently re-derived not only the diagnosis but its standard answers — Pareto sets, scalarisation, decomposition, set-based output — without consistently citing the other communities' priors. A reader who learns MOO through operations research will encounter Tchebycheff scalarisation as a 1986 breakthrough (Steuer 1986); a reader who learns MOO through deep MTL will encounter "preference-conditioned hypernetworks" (Navon et al. 2021) as a 2021 breakthrough; almost no introductory deep-learning text observes that the latter is structurally a population-based decomposition method (MOEA/D, Zhang and Li 2007) lifted to a hypernetwork inference surface.

    A natural response is to ask whether MOO admits a single unifying technical loosening thread comparable to the bilevel optimization community's lower-level-singleton (LLS) assumption, which over thirty years was loosened from singleton through strong-convexity to convexity to stationary-point. The answer, on close reading, is not quite — and the reasons are themselves diagnostic of how MOO has actually evolved. Three or four parallel loosening lines exist:

    (i) The convex-front assumption. Weighted-sum scalarisation, \(\min_x \sum_i w_i f_i(x)\), recovers only the convex-hull-supported portion of the Pareto front; concave regions are inaccessible for any nonnegative \(w\) (Miettinen 1999, §3.1). This assumption was first loosened by weighted Tchebycheff scalarisation \(\min_x \max_i w_i|f_i(x)-z_i^\star|\), which under mild conditions sweeps the entire properly-Pareto front (Steuer 1986; Miettinen Thm 3.4.5 [†caveat-M]). It was loosened a second time by population-based evolutionary methods (Deb 2001; Zitzler 1999) that do not depend on any scalarisation. It was loosened a third time by gradient-based MOO via MGDA, which finds a Pareto-stationary point on the deep-learning loss surface without committing to any scalarisation (Désidéri 2012; Sener and Koltun 2018). And it was loosened a fourth time by weight-interpolation methods (Rewarded Soups, Rame et al. 2023) that approximate Pareto-optimal generalisation across preference space by linear combination of fine-tuned model checkpoints. The explicit loosening claim is in Sener and Koltun (2018): "this workaround [linear combination] is only valid when the tasks do not compete, which is rarely the case."

    (ii) The static-weight assumption. Even within scalarisation, the source of weights has been loosened over time. Hwang and Masud (1979) presented a priori, a posteriori, and interactive variants in their original three-segment classification, which Miettinen (1999) extended to four quadrants by adding the no-preference class. Roijers, Vamplew, Whiteson, and Dazeley (2013) re-derived the same taxonomy fourteen years later for sequential decision problems: "we identify three distinct scenarios in which converting such a problem to a single-objective one is impossible, infeasible, or undesirable." Deep MTL added a fifth modality: weights are learned during training (GradNorm; uncertainty weighting). Preference-conditioned LLM alignment added a sixth: weights are an inference-time input (PHN, Rewarded Soups, Panacea).

    (iii) The single-solution-output assumption. Classical OR returned a single compromise point. EMO returned an approximating set in a single run, by virtue of maintaining a population (Deb 2001). Preference-conditioned hypernetworks returned a continuum of points parametrised by a hypernet input. Panacea (Zhong et al. 2024) returns the same continuum from a single model: "Instead of just learning one such solution, we focus on learning the entire set of Pareto-optimal solutions." The output object expanded from a point to a set to a preference-indexed family of model parameters.

    Three threads run laterally through these loosening lines and reappear in §2, §5, §7, and §8 — we name them here so a reader does not mistake them for one-off digressions. Thread 1 is welfare economics: utilitarian (weighted sum), Rawlsian (max-min and Tchebycheff), Nash bargaining (Nash-MTL), and the Sen capability framework whose dominance order coincides with Pareto's. Thread 2 is information theory and \(f\)-divergence: the choice of divergence in DPO and KL-regularised RLHF acts as a hidden scalarisation between fidelity and mode-coverage. Thread 3 is Goodhart's Law: reward over-optimisation in RLHF is the extremal variant of Manheim and Garrabrant's (2018) Goodhart taxonomy, not the Lucas critique, because the reward model is frozen while the policy distribution shifts. Two further threads — order theory and the cone framing, and decomposition as a design pattern — receive their own subsections in §8.

    The reader's map for what follows. §2 develops the order-theoretic core that the four communities share. §3 exhibits the translation table between their vocabularies. §4-§7 walk each community: evolutionary, gradient MTL, MORL, and RLHF. §8 consolidates the lateral threads. §9 develops critical-reading case studies. §10 lists open problems; §11 is the reading list.

    §2 Core Theory: What Pareto Optimality Actually Buys You#

    Pareto optimality is best understood not as a property of objectives but of the partial order induced by a domination cone \(D\), with three nested efficiency notions (weak, Pareto, strict, proper) and three nested scalarisation classes (weighted sum on convex-supported only; Tchebycheff on every Pareto point; achievement-function with augmentation).

    2.1 Order-theoretic foundation#

    Sawaragi, Nakayama, and Tanino (1985) formalised the multi-objective problem \(\min_X f\) relative to a partial order \(\preceq\) on \(\mathbb{R}^k\). Given a closed pointed convex cone \(D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^k\), the cone-induced dominance is $\(y \preceq_D y' \iff y' - y \in D\setminus\{0\}. \tag{E2}\)$ Efficiency is then minimality of \(f(x)\) under \(\preceq_D\). Geometrically, the cone \(D\) at a point \(y\) is the set of directions you must move to worsen \(y\). Pareto's \(D = \mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\setminus\{0\}\) says "worsening = larger in every coordinate"; weak Pareto uses the dominance relation \(y\prec_w y'\iff y'-y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}) = \mathbb{R}^k_{>0}\) (strict in every coordinate; the underlying cone is the same, but the dominance is the interior relation, since \(\mathbb{R}^k_{>0}\) itself is not closed and therefore violates the closed-pointed assumption of Sawaragi-Nakayama-Tanino). A tilted cone \(D'\) obtained by rotating \(\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\) encodes preference-weighted directions (Yu 1985's preference cones), letting the user say "I trade off task 1 for task 2 at no worse than rate 3:1" by tilting \(D\) in the corresponding direction.

    Three propositions of the order-theoretic framework recur. Existence: under compactness of \(f(X) + D\) and an external-stability condition, efficient solutions exist (Sawaragi et al. 1985, Thm 3.2.1 [†caveat-M]). Scalarisation characterisation: \(\hat{x}\) is \(D\)-efficient if and only if it solves \(\min_X \varphi(f(x))\) for some strictly \(D\)-monotone \(\varphi\). Duality: the dual cone \(D^\star = \{w:\langle w,d\rangle\ge 0\ \forall d\in D\}\) gives the admissible weight directions; for \(D=\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\), \(D^\star = \mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\) recovers nonnegative weights.

    The pedagogical point is that "is this solution Pareto-optimal?" is incomplete without specifying \(D\). ML papers asking the question of MTL solutions tend to leave \(D\) implicit at \(\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\) and to confuse Pareto efficiency with weak efficiency on boundary cases. The order-theoretic view dissolves the confusion: the question is decided by the cone the user chose, often implicitly.

    2.2 Pareto-stationarity and the non-degenerate interior#

    Désidéri (2012, 2014) introduced the multiple-gradient descent algorithm (MGDA) whose stationarity certificate has become the standard target for gradient-based MOO. The certificate is

    \[ \text{$y_0$ is Pareto-stationary} \iff \exists\ \boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\ \text{s.t.}\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_i\nabla J_i(y_0) = 0. \tag{E3} \]

    The common descent direction at a non-stationary point is \(-\mathbf{d}^\star\), where \(\mathbf{d}^\star\) is the min-norm element of the gradient convex hull

    \[ \mathbf{d}^\star = \arg\min_{u\in U}\|u\|,\qquad U = \mathrm{conv}\{\nabla J_i(y_0)\}. \tag{E4} \]

    We adopt Désidéri's convention: \(\mathbf{d}^\star\) denotes the min-norm element itself; the update direction is \(-\mathbf{d}^\star\), along which \(\langle \nabla J_i, -\mathbf{d}^\star\rangle = -\|\mathbf{d}^\star\|^2 < 0\) for every \(i\), i.e., every objective is descended. The convergence guarantee (Désidéri 2012, Thm 1): under \(C^1\) smoothness and Wolfe-condition line search, MGDA iterates have accumulation points, all of which are Pareto-stationary; convexity is not required for this. For nonconvex objectives, Pareto-stationarity is necessary but not sufficient for Pareto-optimality; under (quasi)convexity, stationarity ⇔ Pareto-optimality.

    A persistent error in ML expositions of (E3) is to display the certificate with a boundary example — \(\alpha_1 = 1, \alpha_2 = 0\) — as though that were the interesting case. It is not, because \(\alpha_1=1, \alpha_2=0\) collapses (E3) to single-objective stationarity of \(J_1\) and contributes nothing to the multi-objective story. The instructive case is the interior of the simplex, where every \(\alpha_i \in (0,1)\) and all gradients are genuinely contributing. Désidéri's MGDA-II / Gram-Schmidt construction (Désidéri 2014, eqs. 16-17) gives such an example explicitly. For an orthogonalised gradient family \(\{u_i\}\) (the orthogonality precondition is what gives (E5) its closed form; without it the min-norm-element coefficients of \(\mathrm{conv}\{u_i\}\) have no such simple expression), $$ \mathbf{d}^\star = \sum_i \alpha_i u_i,\qquad \alpha_i = \frac{1}{1+\sum_{j\ne i}|u_i|^2/|u_j|^2}\in(0,1). \tag{E5} $$ Geometrically, when the orthogonal projection of the origin onto the affine hull of \(\{u_i\}\) lies inside the convex hull, the projection equals \(\mathbf{d}^\star\) and the inner product \(\langle u_i, \mathbf{d}^\star\rangle = \|\mathbf{d}^\star\|^2\) is equal across all objectives (Désidéri 2014, eq. 12); along the update direction \(-\mathbf{d}^\star\), every directional derivative \(\langle u_i, -\mathbf{d}^\star\rangle = -\|\mathbf{d}^\star\|^2\) is equal and negative — every objective descends at the same rate. This is the genuine non-degenerate Pareto-stationary configuration, with every coefficient in the open simplex interior. Derivation: orthogonalise the gradient family, project the origin onto its affine hull, and read off the barycentric coordinates; each \(\alpha_i\) is large when \(\|u_i\|\) is small relative to its siblings — small-norm objectives get more weight, which is the precise sense in which MGDA equalises pressure.

    2.3 Cone-dominance and proper efficiency#

    Ehrgott (2005, Ch. 4-5) collects the standard hierarchy of efficiency notions: - Weak Pareto: \(\hat{x}\) is weakly efficient iff there is no \(x\) with \(f_i(x) < f_i(\hat x)\) for every \(i\). Equivalent to the dominance relation \(y\prec_w y' \iff y'-y\in\mathrm{int}(\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0})\). - Pareto (efficient): \(\hat x\) admits no \(x\) with \(f(x)\le f(\hat x)\) and \(f(x)\ne f(\hat x)\). Equivalent to \(D=\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\setminus\{0\}\). - Strict Pareto: \(\hat x\) admits no \(x\ne\hat x\) with \(f(x)\le f(\hat x)\) componentwise. - Proper (Geoffrion 1968): \(\hat x\) is efficient and there exists \(M>0\) such that for every \(i\) and every \(x\) with \(f_i(x) < f_i(\hat x)\), some \(j\) has \(f_j(x) > f_j(\hat x)\) with \(\frac{f_i(\hat x)-f_i(x)}{f_j(x)-f_j(\hat x)} \le M\).

    Proper efficiency rules out pathological "infinite-trade-off" Pareto points and is the natural target for weighted-sum and achievement-function scalarisations. The intuition: an improper Pareto point is one where buying an arbitrarily small improvement in \(f_i\) costs an unbounded loss in \(f_j\). These are usually geometric pathologies (cusps, corners), not points a decision-maker would want. Proper efficiency rules them out by capping the worst trade-off ratio at a finite \(M\). The cone view also opens the door to preference-cone MOO (Yu 1985), which appears in some recent RLHF work where the decision maker's preferences induce a non-standard dominance.

    2.4 The scalarisation zoo#

    Six standard scalarisations cover most of practical MOO; their completeness and failure modes are well documented (Miettinen 1999, Chs 3-4 [†caveat-M]).

    Method Form Coverage of Pareto front Failure mode
    Weighted sum \(\min_x \sum_i w_i f_i\) Convex-supported region only Misses non-convex / concave segments
    Weighted Tchebycheff \(\min_x \max_i w_i\lvert f_i - z_i^\star\rvert\), see (E6) Every properly Pareto point Non-smooth; admits weakly-Pareto solutions
    \(\varepsilon\)-constraint \(\min_x f_j\) s.t. \(f_i \le \varepsilon_i, i\ne j\) Every Pareto point Need to know the front to pick \(\varepsilon\)
    Achievement function \(\min_x\{\max_i (f_i - z_i^{\mathrm{ref}})/w_i + \rho\sum_i (f_i - z_i^{\mathrm{ref}})/w_i\}\), see (E7) Every properly Pareto point Sensitive to \(\rho\) (needs \(\rho\in(0,\rho_0)\)); needs reference point
    Lexicographic sequential \(\min_x f_{\pi(1)}\), fix, \(\min_x f_{\pi(2)}\), … One point per permutation Discontinuous in priorities
    Goal programming \(\min_x \sum_i\|f_i - g_i\|_p\) Not guaranteed Pareto-optimal unless goals infeasible Dominated solutions if goals inside feasible image

    The two equations referenced are $$ g_T(x;w,z^\star) = \max_{i=1,\dots,k} w_i\lvert f_i(x) - z_i^\star\rvert, \tag{E6} $$ $$ g_A(x;w,z^{\mathrm{ref}}) = \max_i (f_i(x) - z_i^{\mathrm{ref}})/w_i + \rho\sum_i (f_i(x) - z_i^{\mathrm{ref}})/w_i, \tag{E7} $$ following Miettinen (1999, Def. 3.4.7), with \(w_i>0\) in the denominator and \(\rho\in(0,\rho_0)\) for problem-dependent \(\rho_0\) tied to the maximum allowable trade-off ratio (Geoffrion's proper-efficiency bound). Under these conditions, minimising \(g_A\) recovers exactly the properly Pareto-efficient solutions regardless of whether \(z^{\mathrm{ref}}\) is feasible.

    The completeness gap between weighted-sum and Tchebycheff (E6) is the operational reason weighted-sum loss-balancing fails on non-convex Pareto fronts. Sener and Koltun's (2018) explicit loosening claim — that weighted-sum is valid only when tasks do not compete — is this fifty-year-old observation transposed into deep learning. The achievement function (E7) further generalises Tchebycheff by allowing an infeasible reference \(z^{\mathrm{ref}}\) and adding a \(\rho\)-augmentation that excludes weakly-Pareto solutions, giving the strongest scalarisation guarantee in the table (Wierzbicki 1980; Miettinen 1999, §3.4 [†caveat-M]).

    2.5 Convexity, support, and the geometry of unreached points#

    A point \(\hat x\) is supported (weighted-sum-reachable) iff \(f(\hat x)\) lies on the boundary of \(\mathrm{conv}\,f(X) + \mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\) that admits a separating hyperplane with nonnegative normal. For concave segments of \(f(X)\), no \(w\succeq 0\) yields \(\hat x\) — these are the duality-gap Pareto points that Tchebycheff and \(\varepsilon\)-constraint do recover. The geometric picture is clean: weighted-sum sees only the upper convex envelope of the objective image; Tchebycheff, by reading off ellipsoidal level sets in the \(z^\star\)-anchored coordinates, can intersect arbitrary regions of the Pareto front.

    The pragmatic consequence is sharper than the geometry alone suggests. A reader trained in single-objective optimisation often hears "weighted sum is fine if you tune \(w\) well" as Lagrangian duality. In single-objective constrained optimisation this is correct under convexity: the Lagrangian dual recovers the primal. In multi-objective optimisation the analogous "weighted-sum dual recovers the Pareto primal" statement is false in general — the Pareto image of a non-convex problem has interior structure that no nonnegative linear functional can probe. Tchebycheff repairs this because \(\max_i\) is itself a non-linear scalarisation; the level sets of \(\max_i w_i\lvert f_i - z_i^\star\rvert\) are non-convex (rectangular orthants in the bi-objective case anchored at \(z^\star\)), and intersecting non-convex level sets with a non-convex Pareto image can pick out the interior segments that linear hyperplanes miss.

    2.6 The completeness theorems and what they buy#

    Three completeness theorems deserve explicit statement because the gap between them is what subsequent communities relax.

    Theorem (weighted sum, classical). If \(f(X) + \mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\) is convex, then \(\hat x\) is properly Pareto-efficient iff there exists \(w\in\Delta^{k-1}_{>0}\) such that \(\hat x\) solves \(\min_x w^\top f(x)\). The convexity assumption is the bottleneck.

    Theorem (Tchebycheff, Miettinen 1999 Thm 3.4.5 [†caveat-M]). For every Pareto-optimal \(\hat x\), there exists \(w\in\mathrm{int}(\Delta^{k-1})\) such that \(\hat x\) solves the weighted Tchebycheff problem \(\min_x\max_i w_i\lvert f_i(x) - z_i^\star\rvert\). No convexity assumption is needed; the price is that the Tchebycheff problem can return weakly-Pareto solutions whose multiplicity must be broken by some secondary criterion.

    Theorem (achievement function, Wierzbicki 1980). For every properly Pareto-efficient \(\hat x\) and any reference point \(z^{\mathrm{ref}}\), there exists \(w\) such that \(\hat x\) solves \(\min_x g_A(x;w,z^{\mathrm{ref}})\) where \(g_A\) is (E7). The \(\rho\)-augmentation excludes weakly-Pareto solutions, giving the strongest single-statement completeness result in the table.

    These three theorems are the algebra behind §5's gradient-direction methods. MGDA's \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\star\) is the weighted-sum certificate in disguise; CAGrad's max-min is structurally a Tchebycheff-like construction in the gradient-conflict regime; achievement-function aspirations appear implicitly in reference-point-based preference articulation methods. The fact that gradient MTL papers rarely cite Wierzbicki or Miettinen — even when re-deriving close variants of (E7) — is one of the more striking gaps in cross-community attribution.

    2.7 Where the order-theoretic view does and does not buy you generality#

    Two limits of the cone-dominance framework should be acknowledged. First, the framework is finite-dimensional and Euclidean by default. Extensions to ordered vector spaces (Jahn 2011) and to lattice-valued MOO exist but are less developed; for set-valued problems where the objective space is a power set (relevant to MORL's \(\mathcal{V}^\star\)), additional structure is needed. Second, the cone \(D\) is presumed constant across the feasible region. State-dependent preferences — where the dominance cone depends on \(x\) or on the current state \(s\) in a sequential decision problem — break the static cone formalism and require either preference cones (Yu 1985) parameterised by a context variable or a richer order-theoretic apparatus.

    These limits matter for §6 and §7. MORL's set-valued \(\mathcal{V}^\star(s)\) is in principle subject to a state-dependent cone (different states can have different preference structures); current MORL formulations almost always assume a constant cone \(D = \mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\). The implicit assumption is rarely interrogated.

    §3 Vocabulary Map: Four Communities, One Object#

    The OR-MCDM, EMO, MTL, and alignment communities use disjoint vocabularies for the same Pareto-optimality concept; we exhibit the translation table. The table is also the first concrete instalment of the §1 thesis: if MOO had a single loosening thread, the row labels would converge on one phrase per row; instead we see four distinct phrasings of conflict, four of solution concept, four of "where the weights come from."     Three rows of the table deserve commentary. The first is the unanimous diagnosis row on conflict / incommensurability: Ehrgott's "conflicting, often incommensurable" (2005), Sener-Koltun's "tasks may conflict, necessitating a trade-off" (2018), Hayes et al.'s "trade-offs between multiple, often conflicting, objectives" (2022), and Panacea's "multi-dimensional and heterogeneous nature of human preferences" (2024) — four different communities saying the same thing.

    The second row is operational divergence on the gradient / scalarisation mechanism. The OR tradition systematically catalogued scalarisation classes by the 1980s; Tchebycheff and achievement methods existed for thirty years before Sener and Koltun (2018) cast MTL as MOO. EMO largely abandoned scalarisation in favour of dominance-and-diversity selection on populations. Deep MTL re-imported scalarisation through MGDA-UB, then layered direction-manipulation methods on top (PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL, FAMO, ConsMTL). RLHF, finally, uses scalarisation by default (Bradley-Terry plus weighted-sum reward) and is now belatedly discovering set-based and preference-conditioned alternatives (Rewarded Soups, MODPO, Panacea, MO-ODPO).

    The third row is the decision-maker question. OR centres the decision maker — Miettinen's four quadrants are organised by when the decision maker articulates preference. EMO often elides them by adopting an a-posteriori stance: produce the front, let someone else pick. Deep MTL elides them by treating the engineer's grid-search as preference articulation. RLHF re-centres the decision maker as the annotator population, with the new wrinkle that there are many of them with potentially heterogeneous preferences — Casper et al. (2023) document how the reward model averages over annotator preferences to output a deterministic scalar reward inconsistent with any single human's preferences, a lossy aggregation that drives much of RLHF's misalignment behaviour.

    Where the four communities agree on the diagnosis but disagree on the mechanism, the disagreements are typically explainable by what their respective evaluation regimes can afford. EMO can afford population-based search because evaluation is cheap (analytic benchmarks like ZDT, DTLZ). Deep MTL cannot afford a population of trained networks; hence the gradient-direction line. RLHF cannot afford to retrain for every preference vector; hence weight interpolation and preference-conditioned policies.

    A useful translation exercise for cross-community readers, and the entry point to the catalogue chapters §4-§7. The OR concept "\(\varepsilon\)-constraint method" is, in MTL vocabulary, "constrain task \(i\ne j\) losses to be below a threshold and minimise task \(j\)" — a formulation rarely seen in deep MTL though it would be conceptually clean for fairness-constrained MTL. The MTL concept "gradient surgery" has no exact OR analogue but is structurally close to projection-based dual methods in non-differentiable optimisation. The RLHF concept "Bradley-Terry reward model" is, in EMO vocabulary, "a learned scalarisation function over preferences" — making explicit a connection the alignment literature seldom makes. The MORL concept "scalarised expected return" is, in OR vocabulary, "a-posteriori weighted-sum scalarisation under stochastic transitions." Once the vocabulary is aligned, the design choices of each community become legible as different settings of the same dial.

    A common confusion to anticipate when reading across communities is the role of the simplex. In MTL the simplex \(\Delta^{k-1}\) hosts computed task weights \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\). In PHN / Rewarded Soups / MODPO the same simplex hosts user preference weights \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) (or \(\lambda\)). In MOEA/D the simplex hosts the decomposition's weight vectors \(w^j\). The objects look identical (vectors in \(\Delta^{k-1}\)); their roles differ. The notation discipline of §0 — \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) for computed, \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) for user, \(\lambda\) for Rewarded-Soups interpolation, \(\lambda^{\mathrm{CMDP}}\) for Lagrange multipliers — is designed to keep these roles separated even when reading source material that does not.

    §4 Evolutionary MOO#

    Evolutionary MOO is not a footnote to gradient MOO; it is the parallel community whose four design pillars — rank-plus-crowding, decomposition, reference points, CMA adaptation — and three principal indicators — HV, IGD\(^+\), \(\Delta m\%\) — MTL still has not adopted. The EMO community is also where §1's loosening line (iii) — the single-solution-output assumption — was first dismantled: a population is the set-valued output, and §4.1's NSGA-II machinery exists to maintain that set rank-then-density. MOEA/D (§4.2) then dismantles loosening line (i) — the convex-front assumption — by routing through Tchebycheff aggregation rather than weighted sum. Two of §1's parallel threads are already visible in this chapter alone.

    4.1 NSGA-II: fast non-dominated sorting plus crowding distance#

    Deb, Pratap, Agarwal, and Meyarivan (2002) introduced NSGA-II to address two complaints against earlier EMO methods: the cubic complexity of naive non-dominated sorting and the lack of elitism in MOGA / NPGA. The complexity improvement, in the authors' words: "Previous multiobjective evolutionary algorithms using nondominated sorting had \(O(MN^3)\) computational complexity (where \(M\) is the number of objectives and \(N\) is the population size). The proposed algorithm has the fast-non-dominated-sort procedure of computational complexity \(O(MN^2)\)" (Deb et al. 2002, abstract). The trick is to maintain for each solution \(p\) the count \(n_p\) of dominators and the set \(S_p\) of solutions dominated by \(p\); computing both for all pairs costs \(O(MN^2)\), and extracting successive fronts then traverses each pair at most twice.

    The crowding-distance formula gives selection pressure for diversity within a Pareto rank. For front \(\mathcal{F}\) and objective \(m\), sort solutions on \(f_m\) ascending; per-objective contributions are $$ d_i^{(m)} = \begin{cases} +\infty & i\in{1,\,|\mathcal{F}|}\ \text{(boundary)},\[2pt] \dfrac{f_m(\mathcal{F}[i+1]) - f_m(\mathcal{F}[i-1])}{f_m^{\max} - f_m^{\min}} & \text{otherwise},\end{cases} \tag{E10} $$ summed across objectives to give \(d_i = \sum_m d_i^{(m)}\). The crowded-comparison operator \(p \prec_n q\) prefers lower rank or, within the same rank, higher crowding distance. This rank-then-density two-tier selection is the template every subsequent EMO algorithm refines.

    4.2 MOEA/D and the Tchebycheff requirement for non-convex fronts#

    Zhang and Li (2007) inverted the EMO design pattern. Instead of running rank-based selection on the full population, MOEA/D decomposes the multi-objective problem into \(N\) scalar subproblems using uniformly distributed weight vectors \(w^1,\dots,w^N \in \Delta^{k-1}\), with each subproblem solved by Tchebycheff aggregation $$ g^{\mathrm{te}}(x\,|\,w^j, z^\star) = \max_i w^j_i\,|f_i(x) - z_i^\star|. \tag{E11} $$ Subproblem \(j\) maintains one current solution; its \(T\)-nearest neighbours in weight space are its candidate exchange partners. The neighbourhood-restricted exchange brings per-generation cost to \(O(N\cdot T)\) instead of \(O(N^2)\).

    The choice of Tchebycheff is not stylistic. Weighted-sum subproblems converge only to supported Pareto points; on non-convex fronts, no choice of \(w\succeq 0\) yields solutions in concave regions. MOEA/D's design therefore requires Tchebycheff (or PBI, penalty-based boundary intersection) when fronts may be non-convex. This is precisely the design pattern that preference-conditioned hypernetworks in deep MTL (PHN, PSL) and weight-interpolation methods in alignment (Rewarded Soups) re-invent, with rare citation back to MOEA/D.

    4.3 NSGA-III and the many-objective curse#

    For \(M\ge 4\) objectives the Pareto-rank signal collapses. For two random points i.i.d. uniform in \([0,1]^M\), the probability that they are mutually non-dominated is $$ \Pr[\text{mutually non-dominated}] = 1 - 2\cdot 2^{-M}, \tag{E12} $$ which is \(1/2\) at \(M=2\) but \(\approx 0.998\) at \(M=10\). The intuition: two points are comparable iff one dominates the other componentwise, which requires all \(M\) coordinate-wise comparisons to go the same way, an event of probability \(2\cdot 2^{-M}\). As \(M\) grows, comparability becomes vanishingly rare. Empirically, almost every member of a randomly initialised population is non-dominated for \(M\ge 4\), and selection pressure on the Pareto-rank signal vanishes (the population-level statement that "almost every member is non-dominated for \(M\ge 4\)" is a separate Bentley-Kung-Thompson 1978 result, not a direct consequence of (E12)). Empirically, NSGA-II's hypervolume on DTLZ benchmarks degrades by 30-60% as \(M\) grows from 3 to 10 (Khare, Yao, Deb 2003; Deb and Jain 2014). Deb and Jain (2014) repaired this by replacing crowding distance with structured reference points on a normalised hyperplane (Das-Dennis simplex-lattice design): each population member associates to its nearest reference line, and environmental selection within each front chooses by perpendicular distance to under-represented references. This restores selection pressure once Pareto rank alone has collapsed.

    The probabilistic argument above is one perspective; a topological argument due to Lê-Loi-Phong (2011) gives a sharper one. The Pareto front in \(M\) objectives generically fills a higher-dimensional manifold whose \(\epsilon\)-covering number grows like \(\epsilon^{-(M-1)}\). Reference-point methods like NSGA-III work in part because they impose a covering discretisation matched to the manifold's dimension. The qualitative distinction is important. "Many-objective" (\(M\ge 4\)) is different from "multi-objective" — the binary dominance relation has lost discriminative power, and methods designed for low \(M\) silently fail. MTL benchmarks rarely have \(M\ge 4\) at the loss level (NYUv2 has three; Cityscapes has three) and so the curse rarely appears in MTL evaluation. But RLHF does face many-objective regimes (helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty, conciseness, faithfulness, formatting, plus task-specific objectives), and the community has not yet adopted the EMO insight.

    4.4 MO-CMA-ES and rotation invariance#

    Igel, Hansen, and Roth (2007) constructed a multi-objective version of the elitist \((1+\lambda)\)-CMA-ES by running \(\mu\) parallel single-objective instances, each with its own search distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_k, \sigma_k^2 \mathbf{C}_k)\). Selection from the \(\mu+\lambda\) candidates uses two-tier ranking: non-dominated sorting first, then a tie-break by either crowding distance or contributing hypervolume \(\Delta_{\mathrm{HV}}(p) = \mathrm{HV}(A) - \mathrm{HV}(A\setminus\{p\})\). From the authors: "Both the elitist single-objective CMA-ES and the MO-CMA-ES inherit important invariance properties, in particular invariance against rotation of the search space, from the original CMA-ES" (Igel et al. 2007). Rotation invariance is the defining advantage: NSGA-II's crowding distance is not rotation-invariant, while MO-CMA-ES is, which lets it handle rotated separable benchmarks like rotated Rastrigin. The MTL community essentially ignores MO-CMA-ES even though hyperparameter MOO — the canonical "expensive evaluation" regime — is exactly its target.

    4.5 Quality indicators: HV, IGD\(^+\), and what MTL skipped#

    The EMO community has spent twenty-five years building a careful indicator toolkit. Two indicators dominate published practice.

    Hypervolume. For a set \(A\subset\mathbb{R}^k\) and reference \(r\succeq A\), $$ \mathrm{HV}(A;r) = \lambda_k\Big(\bigcup_{a\in A}[a, r]\Big), \tag{E8} $$ the \(k\)-dimensional Lebesgue measure of the region dominated by \(A\) and bounded above by \(r\). Hypervolume is the only known unary indicator that is strictly Pareto-monotonic: \(A\prec B \Rightarrow \mathrm{HV}(A;r) > \mathrm{HV}(B;r)\), with every other classical indicator (GD, IGD, spacing, spread) admitting counter-examples (the first formal monotonicity statement is Fleischer 2003, EMO conference proceedings; the weak-compatibility analysis is Zitzler et al. 2003). The price is computational: exact HV in \(d\) objectives is #P-hard in \(d\), with the best exact algorithm (HBDA, Russo and Francisco 2014) at \(O(n^{d/2}\log n)\). Approximation of the least HV contributor is NP-hard (Bringmann and Friedrich 2010). A second subtlety: the empirical density of the \(\mu\) HV-maximising points converges to \(\rho(t)\propto \sqrt{-F'(t)}\) along a bi-objective front \(f_2=F(f_1)\) (Auger, Bader, Brockhoff, Zitzler 2009), so HV systematically under-represents shallow-slope regions — a built-in slope-bias that, while not a correctness issue, biases distributions on knee regions of flat fronts.

    IGD\(^+\). Coello and Cortés (2005) introduced the inverted generational distance $$ \mathrm{IGD}(A;P^\star) = \frac{1}{|P^\star|}\sum_{p\in P^\star}\min_{a\in A}|p - a|2, $$ with \(P^\star\) a sample of the true Pareto front. Ishibuchi, Masuda, Tanigaki, and Nojima (2015) showed by counter-example that IGD is not weakly Pareto-compliant — there exist \(A,B\) with \(A\prec B\) yet \(\mathrm{IGD}(A) > \mathrm{IGD}(B)\). The fix is $$ \mathrm{IGD}^+(A;P^\star) = \frac{1}{|P^\star|}\sum $$ which counts only dominated coordinate gaps and is }\min_{a\in A} d^+(p,a),\quad d^+(p,a) = \sqrt{\sum_i \max(a_i - p_i, 0)^2}, \tag{E9weakly Pareto-compliant — though still not strictly Pareto-compliant the way HV is [†caveat-IGD]. IGD\(^+\) is linear in \(|A|\cdot|P^\star|\) — cheap even for high \(d\) — but requires a reference Pareto set, which is rarely known on real problems and biases the indicator when sampled.

    The standard EMO practice (Ishibuchi et al. 2018) is: HV for \(M\le 4\), IGD\(^+\) for \(M\ge 5\), with \(\ge 31\) independent trials, Wilcoxon rank-sum / sign-rank test at \(\alpha=0.05\), and Bonferroni or Holm correction across configurations. The deep-MTL standard — \(\Delta m\%\) over three seeds against single-task baselines — is well below this bar. \(\Delta m\%\) also conflates distance-to-front and coverage in a way HV/IGD\(^+\) separate. The deep critical-reading lesson (§9) is that MTL should adopt at least IGD\(^+\) as a secondary indicator on many-task settings.

    A subtler point on indicator design that the EMO community has internalised but the MTL community largely has not: Pareto-monotonicity (the property that strictly better sets score strictly better) is not trivial. Most natural-looking indicators fail it. Generational distance, spacing, and spread admit explicit counter-examples where a strictly dominating set scores worse. The fact that HV is the unique known strictly Pareto-monotonic unary indicator is hard-won — it took several years between Zitzler-Thiele 1999's introduction and Fleischer's 2003 formal monotonicity statement. Until and unless an alternative is found, HV is the only benchmark indicator across which two algorithms' rankings can never flip due to indicator artefact.

    4.6 Indicator design and the unreported failure modes#

    Three indicator-design failure modes recur in the EMO literature and would carry into MTL adoption.

    Reference-point bias for HV. HV requires a reference point \(r\succeq A\). Different reference points yield different rankings — a closer reference biases toward solutions far from the utopia, a distant reference biases toward solutions near it. The convention (Ishibuchi et al. 2018) is to use a \(1.1\times\) multiplicative shift beyond the worst observed value per coordinate; this is a heuristic and is sometimes violated. A paper reporting HV without disclosing \(r\) is providing one less number than its appearance suggests.

    Reference-set bias for IGD\(^+\). IGD\(^+\) requires a sampled or estimated reference Pareto set \(P^\star\). For analytic benchmarks (ZDT, DTLZ, WFG) \(P^\star\) is known; for real problems it is estimated. The estimate biases the indicator: a sparse \(P^\star\) gives easy IGD\(^+\) scores, a dense \(P^\star\) gives hard ones. The convention is to disclose \(|P^\star|\) and the estimation method.

    Multi-modal Pareto fronts. Some real-world MOO problems admit multimodal Pareto fronts — multiple disjoint locally-Pareto-optimal regions. HV and IGD\(^+\) do not separately reward coverage of distinct modes; a method that converges fast on one mode can outscore a method that covers all modes more sparsely. Multimodal indicators (Liang et al. 2017) exist but are not standard.

    These limits are not arguments against HV / IGD\(^+\) adoption in MTL; they are arguments for disclosure discipline. The fact that the EMO community has spent twenty-five years cataloguing these failure modes is a credit; importing the indicators without the failure-mode catalogue would import the failure modes without the literacy to recognise them.

    4.7 The Tchebycheff-tropical analogy: a teachable example of informal cross-domain bridges#

    A standing question in EMO and OR is whether the piecewise-linear Pareto front geometry has a clean combinatorial description. Tropical geometry — the min-plus or max-plus semiring \((\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}, \oplus, \odot)\) with \(x\oplus y = \min(x,y), x\odot y = x+y\) (Maclagan and Sturmfels 2015) — is the natural algebraic language for piecewise-linear convex (or concave, with sign conventions) functions: a tropical polynomial \(p(x) = \min_i (c_i + \langle\alpha_i, x\rangle)\) has a graph that is a polyhedral complex, and its "tropical hypersurface" is the locus where the minimum is attained at least twice.

    The Tchebycheff scalarisation (E6) is, in max-plus tropical notation, structurally a tropical polynomial evaluation $$ g_T = \bigoplus_i^{\max} (\log w_i + (f_i - z_i^\star)), $$ and its contour lines are tropical curves (orthants intersected with the simplex). Mikhalkin (2005) introduced tropical curves precisely as zero sets of such polynomials.

    This analogy is suggestive enough that one might expect a published identity-theorem. Six searches combining {tropical, Tchebycheff, max-plus, Pareto front, scalarisation, polynomial} returned no peer-reviewed paper that makes the Tchebycheff-tropical identification as a stand-alone theorem. The closest formal works develop tropical geometry for piecewise-linear neural networks (Maragos, Charisopoulos, Theodosis 2021) or for piecewise-linear curve approximation on weighted lattices (Maragos and Sidiropoulos 2019), but not Pareto fronts. The Tchebycheff-tropical identity is therefore informal cross-domain analogy at present, not a published theorem; it remains a genuine open opportunity to formalise the combinatorial structure (vertex count, active-set regions, sensitivity to weight perturbations) of piecewise-linear Pareto fronts via tropical polytope methods (Joswig 2022) [†caveat-T].

    §5 The Gradient Revolution: MTL as MOO (2018-2025)#

    Sener and Koltun (2018) imported Désidéri's MGDA into deep MTL by way of an upper-bound trick (MGDA-UB); the seven subsequent years have produced (i) projection methods (PCGrad), (ii) max-min within a ball (CAGrad), (iii) bargaining-game updates (Nash-MTL), (iv) scalar-loss-only adaptive weighting (FAMO), (v) bi-level task-parameter routing (ConsMTL), (vi) preference-conditioned hypernetworks (PHN), each addressing a different failure mode of MGDA. This is §1's loosening line (ii) — the static-weight assumption — playing out in the gradient regime: each method picks weights differently. Welfare-economics Thread 1 resurfaces explicitly at §5.4 (Nash bargaining axioms); the structural link between Tchebycheff scalarisation and CAGrad's max-min (§5.3) is Thread 4 (order theory) in local-gradient disguise.

    5.1 MGDA-UB and the cost-regime split#

    The MGDA primitive on a deep network with shared encoder \(\theta^{sh}\) and task-specific heads \(\theta^i\) solves the quadratic program $$ \min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^k \alpha_i \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}i(\theta)\Big|_2^2 $$ over per-task gradients of dimension equal to all shared parameters. Sener and Koltun (2018) observed that if one replaces gradients with respect to the shared encoder \(\theta^{sh}\) by gradients with respect to the encoder output \(Z = g(\theta^{sh}; x)\), a chain-rule bound $$ \Big|\sum_i \alpha_i \nabla}}\mathcal{Li\Big|_2^2 \le \sigma $$ (operator-norm-squared on the Jacobian factor, following Sener-Koltun Prop 2) holds with the Jacobian factor shared across tasks. Minimising the right-hand side over }!\Big(\tfrac{\partial Z}{\partial \theta^{sh}}\Big)^2 \cdot \Big|\sum_i\alpha_i \nabla_Z\mathcal{L}_i\Big|_2^2 \tag{E13\(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) yields the same minimiser, and the replacement vector \(\nabla_Z\mathcal{L}_i\) has dimension equal to the encoder output (typically \(10^3\)-\(10^4\)) instead of full parameter count (\(10^6\)-\(10^8\)).

    The cost regime is critical and is routinely conflated with vanilla MGDA's. Vanilla MGDA needs \(O(k)\) full backward passes — one per task gradient on the full shared parameter graph. MGDA-UB needs one full backward pass on the shared encoder plus \(k\) light backward passes through task-specific heads to compute \(\nabla_Z \mathcal{L}_i\). The dominant cost is one full backward, not \(k\). The conflation matters for scaling claims: the original Sener-Koltun result is that MGDA-UB is feasible at the scale of ResNet-class encoders precisely because of this cost split [†caveat-MGDAUB].

    The convergence theorem (Désidéri 2014, restated by Sener and Koltun): under \(C^2\) smoothness and local convexity, MGDA-UB iterates accumulate at a Pareto-stationary point. The stationarity certificate \(\|\mathbf{d}^\star\| < \text{TOL}\) doubles as a termination test.

    5.2 PCGrad: gradient surgery and the \(k\ge 3\) order issue#

    Yu, Kumar, Gupta, Levine, Hausman, and Finn (2020) proposed projection-based gradient surgery. For each task \(i\) in random order: $$ \mathbf{g}_i \leftarrow \mathbf{g}_i - \frac{\mathbf{g}_i\cdot\mathbf{g}_j}{|\mathbf{g}_j|^2}\mathbf{g}_j \quad \text{whenever } \mathbf{g}_i\cdot\mathbf{g}_j < 0, \tag{E14} $$ applied iteratively across \(j\ne i\). The update direction is then \(\sum_i \mathbf{g}_i\) after surgery.

    PCGrad's \(k=2\) analysis (the paper's Theorem 2) gives a convergence guarantee in the two-task convex case under specific Lipschitz conditions. For \(k\ge 3\) tasks, the random ordering of task projections is itself a tacit acknowledgement that order matters. The paper does not prove order-invariance, and the published critique closest to a cycling proof is Liu et al.'s CAGrad paper (Section 2.2): "PCGrad does not always converge to a Pareto stationary point." No published cycling-failure counter-example with rigorous proof exists; the "alternating projections / POCS" analogy is a useful pedagogical framing for what could fail at \(k\ge 3\), but it is the surveyor's framing, not a literature theorem [†caveat-P].

    The practical takeaway is that PCGrad has empirical effectiveness on \(k\le 3\) MTL benchmarks (NYUv2, CityScapes) but does not extend with theoretical guarantees to many-task regimes. This is the precise gap that CAGrad and Nash-MTL each address differently.

    5.3 CAGrad: max-min in a Euclidean ball, with MGDA and GD as limits#

    B. Liu, X. Liu, X. Jin, P. Stone, Q. Liu (2021), with Peter Stone as senior co-author, proposed $$ \max_{\mathbf{d}}\min_{i\in[k]}\langle \mathbf{g}_i,\mathbf{d}\rangle\ \text{s.t.}\ |\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{g}_0|\le c|\mathbf{g}_0|, \tag{E15} $$ where \(\mathbf{g}_0 = \tfrac{1}{k}\sum_i\mathbf{g}_i\) is the average gradient and \(c\in[0,1)\) is a hyperparameter. The intuition is "find the update direction within a ball around the average that maximises the worst-case task improvement."

    CAGrad has two reduction limits within its declared domain \(c\in[0,1)\). At \(c=0\), the constraint \(\|\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{g}_0\|=0\) collapses to \(\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{g}_0\), recovering ordinary gradient descent on the average loss. As \(c\) grows toward its upper limit, the ball constraint becomes non-binding for the direction (after renormalisation), and the max-min selects the min-norm element of \(\mathrm{conv}\{\mathbf{g}_i\}\) up to step-size choice, recovering MGDA's direction. Liu et al. (2021, §3) derive this MGDA-recovery via the dual; the recommended practical setting is \(c\approx 0.5\). Geometrically: picture the average gradient \(\mathbf{g}_0\) as the centre of a ball of radius \(c\|\mathbf{g}_0\|\). Inside the ball, pick the direction that helps the worst task most. Small ball \(\Rightarrow\) stuck near GD; ball large enough to be non-binding \(\Rightarrow\) fall back on MGDA's min-norm element. The knob \(c\) literally interpolates between "trust the average" and "protect the worst task." For intermediate \(c\), CAGrad interpolates: Theorem 2 in the paper proves it converges to a minimum of the average loss while biasing toward the worst task — a guarantee neither GD nor MGDA provides individually. CAGrad's max-min is structurally a Tchebycheff scalarisation in the gradient-conflict regime: \(\max_\mathbf{d}\min_i\langle\mathbf{g}_i,\mathbf{d}\rangle\) with the ball constraint is, after rescaling, equivalent to a Tchebycheff problem \(\min_\mathbf{d}\max_i\langle-\mathbf{g}_i,\mathbf{d}\rangle\) on the dual ball — i.e., Steuer 1986 in the local-gradient regime.

    5.4 Nash-MTL: bargaining axioms and the disagreement point as normative choice#

    Navon, Shamsian, Achituve, Maron, Kawaguchi, Chechik, Fetaya (2022) applied the Nash bargaining solution to update directions. Each task is a "player" whose payoff from update \(\Delta\theta\) is the predicted loss decrease \(u_i(\Delta\theta) = -\langle \mathbf{g}_i, \Delta\theta\rangle\). The disagreement point is fixed at \(\Delta\theta=0\) (no update, zero payoff). The Nash bargaining solution maximises the product of gains over disagreement: $$ \Delta\theta^\star = \arg\max_{\Delta\theta\in B_\epsilon}\prod_{i=1}^k\langle -\mathbf{g}_i,\Delta\theta\rangle\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\langle-\mathbf{g}_i,\Delta\theta\rangle > 0\ \forall i, \tag{E16} $$ solvable via \(\mathbf{G}^T \mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\alpha} = 1/\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) in dual variables, giving the update \(\Delta\theta^\star = \sum_i \alpha_i \mathbf{g}_i\).

    Nash's bargaining axioms (1950 Econometrica "The Bargaining Problem", with the four-axiom statement refined in Nash 1953) are inherited:

    Axiom Statement in Nash-MTL
    Pareto optimality No other feasible \(\Delta\theta\) dominates the chosen one
    Symmetry No task is privileged a priori
    IIA Removing dominated options does not change the solution
    Affine / scale invariance Rescaling \(\mathcal{L}_i \mapsto c_i\mathcal{L}_i\) leaves \(\Delta\theta^\star\) unchanged

    Nash (1950) proves the Nash-product solution is the unique rule satisfying all four. The vulnerability is the disagreement point. Fixing it at \(\Delta\theta=0\) corresponds to "no update at all." Alternative choices — for instance, the single-task optimum gradient — would shift the Nash solution toward different tasks (specifically, away from those already easy in isolation). The paper does not discuss the disagreement point as a tunable design variable; it is presented as the natural choice. The disagreement point is a normative parameter, not a technical detail. Nash-MTL is fair only with respect to its choice of disagreement; the one-line summary "Nash-MTL is automatically fair" is misleading. The deeper why-question — why Nash bargaining rather than Kalai-Smorodinsky (1975, which replaces IIA with individual monotonicity) or Egalitarian (Kalai 1977, which drops scale invariance) — is essentially historical: Navon et al. (2022) adopted Nash because Nash bargaining is the textbook cooperative solution, but each of the alternatives would induce a different MTL update direction with its own normative content.

    5.5 GradDrop, GradVac, and the projection-filtering-alignment trichotomy#

    Z. Chen, J. Ngiam, Y. Huang, T. Luong, H. Kretzschmar, Y. Chai, D. Anguelov (2020) proposed gradient sign dropout. For each scalar coordinate of the gradient, compute the sign distribution across tasks and probabilistically mask all gradients of the minority sign. Coarser than PCGrad (per-coordinate vs. per-vector) but cheaper.

    Z. Wang, Y. Tsvetkov, O. Firat, Y. Cao (2021) proposed the opposite. GradVac encourages gradient alignment by adding a regulariser pushing pairwise cosine similarities toward task-specific target values (estimated from data). The original application was massively multilingual NMT, where typological proximity gives a natural target similarity matrix.

    The three methods cover three distinct strategies — projection (PCGrad), filtering (GradDrop), and alignment (GradVac) — and the conceptual variety should not be flattened. Naive surveys that group all four under "gradient manipulation" miss that GradVac actively prefers aligned gradients while PCGrad and GradDrop actively remove misaligned components.

    5.6 FAMO: \(O(1)\) from scalar losses#

    B. Liu, Y. Feng, P. Stone, Q. Liu (2023) — four authors at NeurIPS 2023, with FAMO sitting in the same Peter Stone / UT Austin group as CAGrad — pursued an aggressive reduction. The signal for "which task is under-served" is the loss-decrease rate, a function only of the scalar trajectory \(\mathcal{L}_i(t)\). Detection of imbalance does not require knowing how each parameter contributes to \(\mathcal{L}_i\); it requires only knowing that \(\mathcal{L}_i\) is decreasing slowly.

    FAMO maintains weights \(\mathbf{w}_t\) updated by an auxiliary gradient flow using only scalar losses: $$ \mathbf{w}{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \beta\nabla_k(\theta_t)), $$ where }}\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{w};\mathcal{L}_1(\theta_t),\dots,\mathcal{L\(\mathcal{J}\) measures imbalance in loss decrease rates. The model update is then a single backward pass on the weighted sum: \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha\nabla_\theta\sum_i w_i\mathcal{L}_i\). Per-parameter task gradients are never computed separately. Memory and compute are \(O(1)\) in \(k\) aside from the unavoidable \(k\) forward passes for the scalar losses themselves.

    The published main-table headline number on NYUv2 (3 tasks: semantic segmentation, depth, surface normal; MTAN backbone over SegNet) is \(\Delta m\% = -0.13\%\) averaged over 3 seeds [†caveat-FAMO]. The widely-cited \(-0.27\%\) figure (often quoted in secondary surveys) falls within the seed range but is not the reported main-table number. Seed variance is non-trivial in MTL benchmarks; sanity-check numbers should be reported against a fixed seed budget and the variance disclosed.

    5.7 GradNorm: the historical first#

    Z. Chen, V. Badrinarayanan, C.-Y. Lee, A. Rabinovich (2018, ICML) introduced the first adaptive loss-weighting method whose update rule used gradient statistics rather than uncertainty estimates or static priors. Define the relative inverse training rate \(r_i(t) = \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_i(t)/\overline{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}(t)\) where \(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_i(t) = \mathcal{L}_i(t)/\mathcal{L}_i(0)\) is the fractional remaining loss. The target gradient norm for task \(i\) is then \(G_W(t)\cdot[r_i(t)]^\alpha\), and the GradNorm loss-on-loss $$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{grad}}(t) = \sum_i\big|\,|w_i(t)\nabla_W\mathcal{L}_i| - G_W(t)\cdot[r_i(t)]^\alpha\,\big|_1 \tag{E18} $$ is back-propagated to update \(w_i\). The single hyperparameter is the asymmetry coefficient \(\alpha\); the paper recommends \(\alpha=1.5\) for vision. GradNorm predates Sener and Koltun's NeurIPS 2018 paper by months and the gradient-surgery line by two years. It also requires per-task gradient norms, hence \(O(k)\) memory — same as PCGrad and MGDA.

    5.8 Uncertainty weighting as Bayesian scalarisation#

    Kendall, Gal, Cipolla (2018, CVPR) treated each task's loss as a likelihood with homoscedastic noise variance \(\sigma_i^2\) and derived maximum-likelihood loss $$ \mathcal{L}(\theta,\boldsymbol{\sigma}) = \sum_i \frac{1}{2\sigma_i^2}\mathcal{L}_i(\theta) + \log\sigma_i, \tag{E17} $$ where the \(\log\sigma_i\) term prevents the trivial \(\sigma_i\to\infty\) solution. The effective weights are \(w_i = 1/(2\sigma_i^2)\). The Bayesian framing provides a principled choice — high aleatoric uncertainty tasks get down-weighted — but it commits to a single point on the Pareto front (the maximum-likelihood point), not the front itself. Uncertainty weighting is thus a scalarisation method with a Bayesian justification, not a front-traversing method. It is distinct from MGDA, which also produces a single point but via Pareto-stationarity rather than likelihood.

    5.9 The Three-Way Trade-off#

    L. Chen, H. Fernando, Y. Ying, T. Chen (2023, NeurIPS; extended JMLR version 2024) gave the first formal trade-off result for adaptive weighting. The paper's MoDo algorithm (stochastic MGDA variant) has a step size \(\gamma\) in the dynamic weight update (distinct from the model parameter learning rate \(\alpha\)) that controls three quantities simultaneously: convergence to a conflict-avoidant direction (requires \(\gamma\to 0\) slowly), convergence to empirical Pareto stationarity (requires \(\gamma\) summable), and population (generalisation) risk (optimal at \(\gamma=\Theta(1/\sqrt{n})\)). Theorem 3.1 proves no choice of \(\gamma\) simultaneously optimises all three. Aggressive conflict-avoidance sacrifices generalisation; optimal generalisation sacrifices conflict-avoidance.

    This is the deep reason why MGDA, PCGrad, and Nash-MTL often fail to outperform careful static weighting at scale: static weights correspond to \(\gamma=0\) in the dynamic-update scheme, which lies on the Pareto frontier of the three-way trade-off rather than off it. A partial vindication of "just tune weights by grid search."

    5.10 ConsMTL: bi-level routing through task-specific parameters#

    X. Qin, X. Wang, J. Yan (2025, CVPR) at SJTU proposed a bi-level formulation where the upper level performs gradient aggregation on shared parameters (similar to MGDA) and the lower level introduces an auxiliary loss on task-specific parameters that guides per-task shared-parameter gradients toward the joint update vector. The architectural insight is that task-specific parameters, which most MTL methods leave unmanaged, are repurposed as conflict-reduction levers.

    The headline number on NYUv2 (MTAN backbone) is \(\Delta m\% = -0.59\%\) — ConsMTL is reported as the only method achieving negative \(\Delta m\%\) on NYUv2 in the paper's main table, i.e., outperforming single-task baselines on average across all three tasks [†caveat-ConsMTL]. A separate figure of \(-1.42\%\) that circulates in secondary citations belongs to a different benchmark in the same paper (possibly CityScapes or CelebA, where ConsMTL also outperforms STL); it is not the NYUv2 number. This kind of cross-benchmark contamination is a recurring failure mode in MTL reporting: authors give one headline number, surveys then attach it to whichever benchmark the survey happens to be discussing.

    5.11 PHN, PSL, and the preference-conditioned line#

    A. Navon, A. Shamsian, G. Chechik, E. Fetaya (2021, ICLR) — four authors, three of whom also appear on Nash-MTL — introduced Pareto Hypernetworks. A hypernetwork \(h_\phi:\Delta^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}^P\) maps a preference vector \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) to model parameters \(\theta(\boldsymbol{\omega}) = h_\phi(\boldsymbol{\omega})\). Training optimises \(\phi\) on a preference-conditioned scalarisation \(\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(h_\phi(\boldsymbol{\omega})) = \sum_i \omega_i\mathcal{L}_i\) (or a non-linear scalarisation for non-convex fronts) over \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\alpha)\) with concentration \(\alpha<1\) to bias toward simplex boundaries (Navon et al. 2021); the choice of \(\alpha\) controls which regions of the front PHN samples preferentially. Inference is constant-time: feed in the preference, read out the model. The paper reports 10-50× speed-up over naive multi-run sweeping.

    Geometrically: instead of training one model per preference vector and storing \(N\) checkpoints, PHN trains a single map from the preference simplex into parameter space. The hypernet's output for \(\boldsymbol{\omega}=(1,0,\dots,0)\) is the parameters of a model that optimises objective 1 alone; for an interior \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) it is a model optimising the corresponding trade-off. The simplex is the index of the Pareto family, and \(h_\phi\) is the inference-time lookup table. This is the structural move that MOEA/D made in 2007 with \(N\) weight vectors and a population, lifted to a continuous index via neural function approximation.

    Pareto Set Learning (PSL) — Xi Lin, Z. Yang, X. Zhang, Q. Zhang (2022, NeurIPS), with Qingfu Zhang (the MOEA/D author) as senior — is operationally similar but addresses a different problem. PSL's network outputs decision variables directly, not model parameters, and is intended for expensive multi-objective optimisation problems (Bayesian Optimization) where evaluating \(f\) requires simulation rather than network training. The foundational PSL-for-MTL work is Lin et al.'s "Controllable Pareto Multi-Task Learning" (arXiv:2010.06313, 2020); secondary surveys occasionally conflate this with the 2022 PSL paper, but the two have different target object structures and should be kept separate.

    The intellectual lineage from MOEA/D (decomposition into preference-indexed subproblems with niche cooperation) through PHN (preference-indexed parameter mapping) to Rewarded Soups (preference-indexed weight interpolation, §7.4) is direct, but rarely cited by the deep-learning side.

    A persistent objection to the entire preference-conditioned line is that humans cannot meaningfully express preference weights \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) at inference time. Behavioural-economics evidence (Tversky-Kahneman; Lichtenstein-Slovic) suggests humans cannot stably report cardinal trade-offs even between two attributes, let alone \(k\ge 4\). In practice the preference-indexed parameter family is a design freedom for the system designer (admin dashboards, A/B testing, per-deployment policy selection) rather than the end-user — at which point the methods collapse, operationally, to a static-weight regime of the kind §1's loosening line (ii) was meant to leave behind. Elicitation-literature work (Chajewska-Koller-Parr 2000 on utility elicitation; Boutilier on POMDP-based preference learning) provides one principled response: even noisy elicited preferences can be useful in expectation. The methods of §5.11 and §7.3 do not currently engage this elicitation literature; the gap is acknowledged here and left to future work.

    5.12 The "common descent cone" framing — surveyor's synthesis, not literature#

    Throughout this section the geometry that unifies MGDA, CAGrad, PCGrad, GradDrop, and the rest is the set of common descent directions — vectors \(\mathbf{d}\) with \(\langle\mathbf{d}, \mathbf{g}_i\rangle < 0\) for all \(i\), which exists iff \(0\notin\mathrm{conv}\{\mathbf{g}_i\}\), i.e., the current point is not Pareto-stationary. This set is a cone (the polar of the convex hull of gradients, intersected with the negative half-space). The convenient pedagogical phrase "common descent cone" — which renders MGDA as picking the min-norm vector of the cone, CAGrad as picking the worst-case-improvement vector within a Euclidean ball around the average, PCGrad as a sequential-projection construction, and so on — is a useful synthesis. But the phrase itself is not in the literature. Fliege and Svaiter (2000) speak of the "common descent direction set" or "set of common descent directions"; Désidéri (2012) speaks of the "convex hull of gradients"; Lin et al.'s recent gradient-MOO survey (arXiv:2501.10945) describes methods as "imposing directional constraints on the update vector." None of these use a unified "cone geometry" framing. We adopt the phrase because it is pedagogically helpful, but flag it as surveyor synthesis rather than literature citation [†caveat-C].

    §6 Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning#

    The vector-Bellman equation predates the ML-MOO synthesis by twenty years (White 1982), and the exponential Pareto-front blowup under stochastic transitions (Wiering and de Jong 2007) is the structural obstruction that drives modern MORL toward utility-based scalarisation or restricted policy classes. This chapter extends §2's order-theoretic core — the cone-induced partial order on \(\mathbb{R}^k\) (Thread 4) — from static feasible regions to sequential decision: vector-valued return, set-valued Bellman, \(\mathcal{V}^\star(s)\subset\mathbb{R}^k\) as a state-indexed family of non-dominated vectors. The structural cost is that the candidate-set of non-dominated returns can grow combinatorially with horizon (§6.2). Roijers 2013's re-derivation of Miettinen's four quadrants in MDP language (§6.3) is the chapter's other landmark — independent rediscovery of an a-priori-vs-a-posteriori taxonomy fourteen years later, exactly the cross-community parallelism §1 named.

    6.1 White 1982: vector Bellman and the set Bellman operator#

    D. J. White's 1982 Journal of Mathematical Analysis and Applications paper "Multi-objective infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision processes" is the earliest published formulation of a vector-valued Bellman recursion: $$ \mathbf{V}^\pi(s) = \mathbf{r}(s,\pi(s)) + \gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\,|\,s,\pi(s))\mathbf{V}^\pi(s'), \tag{E19} $$ with \(\mathbf{V}^\pi\in\mathbb{R}^k\). White's dynamic-programming algorithm replaces the scalar Bellman operator by a set-valued one. Let \(\mathcal{V}^\star(s)\) denote the set of non-dominated value vectors achievable from \(s\); the set Bellman operator is $$ \mathcal{V}^\star(s) = \mathrm{ND}\Big(\bigcup_{a}\Big{\mathbf{r}(s,a) + \gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\,|\,s,a)\mathbf{v} : \mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}^\star(s')\Big}\Big), \tag{E20} $$ where \(\mathrm{ND}(\cdot)\) extracts the non-dominated subset. For deterministic MDPs \(\mathcal{V}^\star(s)\) is tractable; under stochastic transitions, the convex combinations introduced by the transition kernel cause the set to grow.

    6.2 The exponential Pareto-front blowup#

    Wiering and de Jong (2007, ADPRL) showed that for stochastic MOMDPs the set of non-dominated stationary policies can grow exponentially in the horizon, even for finite state-action spaces. Their CON-MODP algorithm avoids the blowup only under deterministic-transition restriction. The structural reason is that any randomisation over policies — and hence over their value vectors — introduces convex combinations of value vectors as additional candidates for non-domination, so the non-dominated set grows combinatorially.

    This is the structural fact that distinguishes MORL from offline MOO. In MOO over a static feasible region one has a fixed Pareto front to approximate; in MORL the very set of candidate solutions (induced by the stochastic dynamics) explodes with horizon. Concretely, stochastic transitions let two deterministic policies share a state and split into a Pareto-incomparable mixture; the mixture is a new non-dominated value vector, and mixtures of mixtures appear at the next step. The structural analogue in statistical mechanics is replica symmetry breaking (Mézard-Parisi-Virasoro 1987) for spin-glass optimisation, where the same combinatorial explosion drives an ultrametric-tree structure on the optimum set; Tarapore-Mouret-Doncieux (2016) have used this connection for diversity maintenance in EMO, though the formal MORL-to-RSB mapping remains unpublished. Hence the modern MORL community typically retreats to one of three restrictions: (i) utility-based scalarisation (Roijers et al. 2013) that effectively projects \(\mathcal{V}^\star(s)\) down to a single scalar via a user utility function \(u:\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}\); (ii) restricted policy classes (deterministic, single-action-per-state) under which \(\mathcal{V}^\star(s)\) remains polynomial; or (iii) \(\epsilon\)-domination relaxations (Papadimitriou-Yannakakis 2000), which give polynomial-size coverings of the Pareto front at known approximation cost.

    6.3 Roijers 2013 and Hayes 2022: the utility-based taxonomy#

    Roijers, Vamplew, Whiteson, and Dazeley (2013) — the canonical modern MORL survey — recast the MORL design space around the user-utility function \(u\) and three scenarios in which converting to a single objective is impossible, infeasible, or undesirable: (i) unknown weights (no \(u\) at decision time, need a coverage set of policies); (ii) decision support (must expose the trade-offs to a downstream human decision maker); (iii) known weights but multi-policy required (legal or fairness constraints).

    From Roijers et al. (2013): "we identify three distinct scenarios in which converting such a problem to a single-objective one is impossible, infeasible, or undesirable." Within each scenario the survey distinguishes the applicable scenario, the nature of the scalarisation function (linear vs. monotone non-linear), and the type of policies considered (single policy, convex coverage, full Pareto front). The optimal solution structure varies: "the nature of an optimal solution ... can be a single policy, a convex hull, or a Pareto front." This is the Miettinen four-quadrant taxonomy specialised to sequential decision-making, derived fourteen years later by a different community — and the structural isomorphism is striking.

    6.4 SER vs. ESR#

    Hayes, Rǎdulescu, Bargiacchi, Källström, Macfarlane, Reymond, Verstraeten, Zintgraf, Dazeley, Heintz, Howley, Irissappane, Mannion, Nowé, Ramos, Restelli, Vamplew, Roijers (2022) — eighteen authors, the largest authorship list in this survey's bibliography — codified two important distinctions. Scalarised Expected Return (SER) computes \(\mathbb{E}[u(\mathbf{V}^\pi)]\) — utility of the expected return vector. Expected Scalarised Return (ESR) computes \(u(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{V}^\pi])\) — expected utility along trajectories. For non-linear \(u\) these differ by Jensen's inequality (and by an additional gap from risk preferences). The choice is consequential: in safety-critical RL, ESR captures risk-aversion that SER cannot, because SER averages over uncertainty before applying utility, smoothing out tail behaviour. Hayes et al. (2022) catalogue empirical regimes in which each is appropriate.

    Hayes et al. also enumerate four design axes that Roijers' three-scenario taxonomy did not cover explicitly: single-policy vs. multi-policy returned; deterministic vs. stochastic optimal; transient vs. stationary regime; and whether the utility function is provided ex ante or learned during interaction. The eighteen-author author list reflects the fact that this is a community survey, not a single laboratory's product.

    6.5 Where MORL connects back to gradient MTL and to alignment#

    Two connections are worth flagging. First, the structural distinction between SER and ESR — applying the utility outside or inside the expectation — is the analogue, in MORL terms, of the distinction in deep MTL between scalarising losses (SER-like: weighted-sum of losses, then optimise) and gradient-direction methods (closer to picking a per-step ESR-aware direction). Second, Safe RLHF (§7) is essentially a constrained MORL problem with helpfulness as reward and harmfulness as cost, and the primal-dual machinery has its roots in Altman's (1999) constrained MDP framework, which we develop fully in §7.6.

    The MORL community is in a sense the maturest of the four communities on questions of theory — set-valued Bellman, exponential blowup, SER/ESR distinction, utility-based taxonomy — but the smallest of the four on questions of deployment at scale. The deep-MTL and alignment communities, by contrast, deploy at scale but have not yet imported MORL's structural diagnostics.

    6.6 What "linear scalarisation is insufficient" actually means in MORL#

    The single most-quoted MORL diagnostic is Hayes et al.'s (2022) observation that the majority of RL research "either assumes only a single objective, or that multiple objectives can be adequately handled via a simple linear combination," and that such approaches "may oversimplify the underlying problem and hence produce suboptimal results." The phrasing is careful — the claim is not that linear scalarisation is always wrong, but that it produces suboptimal results in three distinct senses.

    First, coverage: a linear scalarisation \(u(\mathbf{v}) = w^\top\mathbf{v}\) recovers only the convex coverage set of policies, the same way weighted-sum in static MOO recovers only the convex hull of the Pareto front. Non-convex regions of the policy-value-vector image are unreachable.

    Second, risk: linear scalarisation is risk-neutral by construction; ESR with non-linear \(u\) encodes risk attitudes that linear \(u\) cannot. Hayes et al. give concrete examples in catastrophe-avoidance settings where the optimal policy under any linear \(u\) is dominated by a non-linear-utility-optimal policy when the operational regime demands tail-aversion.

    Third, fairness across objectives: linear scalarisation implements utilitarian aggregation; max-min or Tchebycheff-like utilities implement Rawlsian aggregation. The two can produce different optimal policies even on convex coverage sets.

    The recurring "linear scalarisation is fine if you tune \(w\) well" objection misses all three. The MORL community has, over fifteen years, accumulated case studies in deep-water reservoir management, traffic light control, and inventory management where the empirical gap between linear and non-linear utility is large enough to matter operationally. The lesson re-imports cleanly into RLHF: the standard weighted-sum reward in alignment is the analogue of the linear-scalarisation regime MORL spent fifteen years working past.

    §7 RLHF and LLM Alignment as MOO#

    RLHF is the largest live deployment of MOO. The scalar Bradley-Terry reward is a misspecified aggregator (Casper et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2024); the relevant impossibility is not Arrow's classical theorem but the Ge-Halpern 2024 RLHF-specific impossibility in the linear social-choice framework; and the reward over-optimisation phenomenon is Goodhart's Law (extremal variant), not the Lucas critique. The chapter is also where §1's loosening line (ii) — when preferences are articulated — gets its sharpest modern instance: MODPO encodes preference weights at training time, Rewarded Soups as inference-time interpolation, MO-ODPO as prompt tokens, Panacea as a single model parametrising the full preference simplex. All three lateral threads converge here: welfare-economics Thread 1 in §7.7-§7.9, information-theoretic Thread 2 in §7.11-§7.12, Goodhart Thread 3 in §7.13.

    7.1 DPO and the implicit-reward identity#

    Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Manning, Ermon, Finn (2023, NeurIPS) — six authors — derived Direct Preference Optimisation from the KL-constrained RL objective $$ \max_\pi\mathbb{E}{x,y}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,|\,\pi), $$ whose closed-form optimum is }\(\pi^\star(y\,|\,x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\,|\,x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\). Inverting gives the implicit reward $$ r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\,|\,x)} + \beta\log Z(x). \tag{E23} $$ Substituting (E23) into the Bradley-Terry likelihood cancels \(Z(x)\) (which depends only on \(x\), not on \((y_w,y_l)\)), yielding the DPO loss $$ \mathcal{L}{\mathrm{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi\theta(y_w\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\,|\,x)} - \beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\,|\,x)}\big)\Big]. \tag{E24} $$ The paper's title — "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" — encapsulates the result. (E23) is the single most useful equation for connecting DPO to subsequent multi-objective variants: MODPO, \(f\)-DPO, MO-ODPO all begin by manipulating it. The geometric content is that any policy that is optimal for some reward under KL-constrained RL must look like a softmax tilt of \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\); the implicit-reward identity inverts this — given a trained policy, you can read off the reward that justifies it. DPO turns this around again: parameterise the reward implicitly via \(\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\) and learn the policy directly on preference data.

    7.2 MODPO: linear-margin multi-objective DPO#

    Zhou, Liu, Shao, Yue, Yang, Ouyang, Qiao (2024, Findings of ACL) generalised DPO to multiple linearly-combined rewards. The assumption is that rewards decompose linearly across \(k\) objectives, \(r_w(x,y) = w^\top r(x,y) = \sum_i w_i r_i(x,y)\), for user-chosen \(w\in\Delta^{k-1}\). Under this assumption the MORLHF optimum policy for weight \(w\) satisfies a modified implicit-reward identity, and MODPO encodes the off-objective rewards as a margin term in a single cross-entropy step: $$ \mathcal{L}{\mathrm{MODPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\tfrac{1}{w_k}[\beta\log\tfrac{\pi\theta(y_w\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\,|\,x)} - \beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\,|\,x)} - m_w(x,y_w,y_l)]\big)\Big] $$ where \(m_w\) encodes the linear combination of already-learned reward heads for the \(k-1\) marginal objectives. The claim is the same optimum as MORLHF (PPO with \(w\)-weighted reward) at roughly one-third the compute.

    The linearity assumption is significant and tends to be elided. Linear weighting of rewards has the same scalarisation limitation as weighted-sum in classical MOO: it recovers only the convex-supported Pareto front of \((r_1,\dots,r_k)\). Non-linear human utility — concave in helpfulness past saturation, or strongly lexicographic in safety — cannot be expressed in MODPO's form. This is a structural restriction inherited from view E in our taxonomy.

    7.3 MO-ODPO: prompt-conditional online DPO#

    Gupta, Sullivan, Li, Phatale, Rastogi (2025, AAAI) introduced an alternative where (a) a single policy \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\,|\,x,w)\) takes the weight vector \(w\) as a prompt token sequence at inference, so no parameter interpolation and no separate models per \(w\) are needed, and (b) preference pairs are generated by the current policy each step, so the Bradley-Terry data does not drift off-policy as it tends to in offline MODPO. The empirical claim — Pareto domination of Rewarded Soups, P-MORL, and Rewards-in-Context on Anthropic-HH and Reddit TL;DR — is non-trivial because MORLHF baselines often cluster, and "even spacing across the Pareto frontier" is a strong success criterion. MO-ODPO can be read as the alignment community's reinvention of preference-conditioned hypernetworks: the conditioning is in the prompt rather than in the hypernet weights, but the design pattern is the same.

    7.4 Rewarded Soups: linear weight interpolation in parameter space#

    Rame, Couairon, Dancette, Gaya, Shukor, Soulier, Cord (2023, NeurIPS) proposed the simplest multi-objective alignment baseline. Fine-tune \(k\) separate models \(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k\) each against a single reward \(R_i\), then at inference linearly interpolate the weights (not the rewards or outputs): $$ \theta_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i\theta_i,\quad \lambda\in\Delta^{k-1}. \tag{E25} $$ The empirical claim is that \(\theta_\lambda\) approximately Pareto-dominates the MORL policy trained with reward \(\sum_i\lambda_i R_i\). The theoretical basis is linear mode connectivity (Frankle et al. 2020) — the observation, made rigorous under quadratic loss assumptions, that solutions of independent fine-tunes from a shared pre-trained initialisation remain linearly connected in weight space, so convex combinations of weights have well-behaved losses.

    The contrast with MODPO is sharp. MODPO trains a single model that knows \(w\) analytically via a margin term in the loss. Rewarded Soups trains \(k\) models and combines them at inference time without any retraining for new \(\lambda\). The Rewarded-Soups thesis is "embracing the heterogeneity of diverse rewards by following a multi-policy strategy" with the empirical hook "the weights remain linearly connected when fine-tuned on diverse rewards from a shared pre-trained initialization." This is, conceptually, exactly the MOEA/D design pattern lifted to weight space.

    Rewarded Soups is the natural baseline against which every other multi-objective alignment method should be reported, and is too often omitted from comparison tables.

    7.5 Safe RLHF as a CMDP#

    Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, Yang (2024, ICLR) — eight authors at PKU Alignment / BAAI, with Yaodong Yang as the corresponding last author. The middle names are Ji, Xu, Liu (Jiaming Ji, Xinbo Xu, Mickel Liu); secondary surveys occasionally substitute the wrong names here.

    Safe RLHF is a two-reward CMDP. Helpfulness reward \(R_\phi\) and cost (harmfulness) model \(C_\psi\) are trained from separate annotator preferences. The constrained objective is solved by PPO-Lagrangian: $$ \max_\theta\mathbb{E}{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi\theta}[R_\phi(x,y)] - \lambda^{\mathrm{CMDP}}\big(\mathbb{E}[C_\psi(x,y)] - d\big),\quad \lambda^{\mathrm{CMDP}} \leftarrow [\lambda^{\mathrm{CMDP}} + \eta_\lambda(\mathbb{E}[C_\psi] - d)]_+ . $$ A single Lagrange multiplier is updated via gradient ascent on the dual — standard primal-dual CMDP machinery in the policy-gradient inner loop. The notation \(\lambda^{\mathrm{CMDP}}\) disambiguates from Rewarded Soups' interpolation coefficient.

    The reported Elo improvements (Beaver-v3 versus Alpaca-7B base) are paired by evaluator: helpfulness Elo gain — GPT-4 evaluator +244.91 and human evaluator +363.86; harmlessness Elo gain — GPT-4 evaluator +268.31 and human evaluator +237.98. The harmful-response rate fell from 53.08% (Alpaca-7B) to 2.45% (Beaver-v3 after three Safe-RLHF rounds). The paper's claim that "GPT-4 and human evaluators are almost consistent" depends on which axis one looks at. Secondary citations sometimes report unpaired figures (e.g., "+364 / +238" without a specified evaluator) that mix evaluators across helpfulness and harmlessness; the paired numbers above are the ones to cite.

    7.6 CMDP foundations: Altman 1999 and the duality-gap question#

    The textbook reference for the optimisation framework Safe RLHF inhabits is Altman (1999), Constrained Markov Decision Processes. Constrained MDPs add expected-cost constraints to the standard MDP; Altman's key reformulation is that the occupancy measure — the discounted state-action visitation density induced by a policy — makes both the reward and the cost constraint linear in this measure, turning a hard policy-search problem into a linear program in a different variable. The CMDP is $$ \max_\pi\mathbb{E}^\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)\Big]\ \text{s.t.}\ \mathbb{E}^\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t c_i(s_t,a_t)\Big]\le b_i,\ i=1,\dots,k. \tag{E21} $$ Altman's central reformulation defines the occupancy measure \(\rho^\pi(s,a) := \sum_t \gamma^t\Pr^\pi(s_t=s,a_t=a)\). Both reward and cost become linear functionals of \(\rho^\pi\), and the feasible set of occupancy measures is a convex polytope in the finite-state-action setting. The CMDP becomes a linear program whose dual gives Lagrange multipliers \(\lambda_i^\star\). Theorem 3.6 of Altman (1999): for finite state/action CMDPs with the standard occupancy-measure parameterisation, strong duality holds (zero duality gap), and an optimal stationary policy exists that may need randomisation among at most \(k\) deterministic policies.

    The duality-gap question is not automatic for function-approximated policies, which is the actual RLHF setting. Paternain, Calvo-Fullana, Chamon, Ribeiro (NeurIPS 2019) showed that zero duality gap holds for sufficiently rich policy classes — the result that Safe RLHF papers cite implicitly when claiming primal-dual convergence. The implicit citation does substantial work: without Paternain et al., Safe RLHF's primal-dual convergence is not theoretically guaranteed at scale [†caveat-CMDP].

    The middle-ground reference is Tessler, Mankowitz, Mannor (2019, ICLR), Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO). The policy gradient is $$ \nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{RCPO}} = \mathbb{E}[(R - \lambda^{\mathrm{CMDP}} C)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta];\qquad \dot\lambda^{\mathrm{CMDP}} \propto (\mathbb{E}[C] - d). \tag{E22} $$ Convergence holds under standard Robbins-Monro step-size conditions plus an existence-of-feasible-policy assumption. RCPO inherits Borkar two-timescale theory; sample-complexity is \(\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-4})\) for \(\epsilon\)-feasible-and-optimal under tabular or linear policies (Achiam et al. 2017 CPO; Yu et al. 2019; Ding et al. 2021). Safe RLHF is essentially RCPO with the helpfulness reward and the harmfulness cost replacing the synthetic CMDP rewards and costs of the original.

    A short signpost before the social-choice triple §7.7-§7.9. The previous six subsections developed the optimisation apparatus (DPO, MODPO, MO-ODPO, Rewarded Soups, Safe RLHF, CMDP foundations); the next three switch to the aggregation apparatus — how preferences from many annotators become a single reward signal. The technical level shifts from KL-regularised RL to social choice; the unifying question stays "what scalarisation is implicit here, and what does it cost."

    7.7 Arrow's theorem and what RLHF actually violates#

    Arrow's (1951) impossibility theorem is widely invoked in the alignment literature as a generic warning. The precise statement requires three structural conditions: (i) preferences are ordinal (rank-only, no cardinal scale); (ii) there are \(\ge 3\) alternatives; (iii) there are finitely many voters. Under these conditions, no social welfare function — mapping a profile of complete-and-transitive ordinal preferences to a complete-and-transitive social ranking — simultaneously satisfies the four axioms U (unrestricted domain), IIA (independence of irrelevant alternatives), P (weak Pareto), D (non-dictatorship).

    Bradley-Terry (1952), which underlies RLHF reward modelling, does not operate under condition (i). The BT model \(\Pr(y_w\succ y_l\,|\,x) = \sigma(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l))\) produces a cardinal score \(r\) from ordinal pairwise data. This is exactly what Arrow's U-domain forbids in classical social-choice theory; BT enforces a transitive, complete, cardinally calibrated underlying utility. Arrow's domain assumes only ordinal rankings with no implied cardinal structure. So RLHF does not literally violate Arrow; it operates outside Arrow's scope by adopting a stronger (parametric, cardinal) model. The escape is via Harsanyi's (1955) result that cardinal utility aggregation is consistent with Arrow's axioms once cardinal information is added.

    Arrow 1951 (classical) Bradley-Terry / RLHF
    Input Ordinal individual preferences Ordinal pairwise data
    Output Ordinal social ranking Cardinal score \(r\)
    Domain All preference profiles Transitive, complete, BT-compatible profiles
    Aggregation Generic rank-aggregation Parametric (BT MLE)
    Status Impossibility theorem applies Operates outside Arrow's scope (Harsanyi 1955 bypass)

    The empirical assumption underlying BT — that preferences arise from a single latent cardinal scale shared across annotators — is itself questionable (Eric Zhao 2025, arXiv:2505.19964; Liu et al. 2024 arXiv:2411.04991). But its failure, when it does, is not Arrow's impossibility. The precise statement is: RLHF does not violate Arrow's theorem, because it does not satisfy the assumptions of Arrow's domain; the correct impossibility is Ge-Halpern (§7.8) [†caveat-Arrow].

    7.8 Ge-Halpern 2024: the right RLHF impossibility#

    Ge, Halpern, Micha, Procaccia, Shapira, Vorobeychik, Wu (2024, NeurIPS) — seven authors including Daniel Halpern and Ariel Procaccia — recast RLHF reward learning as a rank-aggregation problem in a linear social choice framework. The "linearity" comes from RLHF's reward parameterisation restricting the admissible aggregation rules.

    A linear rank-aggregation rule, in Ge-Halpern's sense, is one whose score for an alternative is a linear function of summary statistics over pairwise comparisons; Bradley-Terry MLE qualifies because its score depends linearly on win-counts. The headline result: any such rule that depends only on majority preferences must violate Pareto Optimality. Bradley-Terry and broad generalisations of it (the entire maximum-likelihood class for pairwise comparison data) fall in this class, and so they fail Pareto Optimality in this axiomatic setup. Importantly, this contrasts sharply with classical social choice, where many rules trivially satisfy Pareto Optimality.

    Ge-Halpern propose leximax Copeland subject to Pareto Optimality as a constructive alternative — a linear rank aggregation rule that satisfies Pareto Optimality, Pairwise Majority Consistency, majority consistency, and winner monotonicity. (Copeland: rank alternatives by net pairwise wins. Leximax: break ties by largest deficit, then second-largest, and so on lexicographically.) The proposal serves both as an axiomatic counter-example to BT's optimality and as the start of a positive theory: rules can be designed that satisfy specific axiom bundles in the cardinal-parametric regime.

    This is the precise replacement for the loose "RLHF violates Arrow" gloss that occasionally circulates in alignment writing. The impossibility is not Arrow's classical theorem; it is the Ge-Halpern RLHF-specific impossibility in the linear-social-choice framework, with Bradley-Terry-class rules occupying the impossibility's prohibited side.

    A fourth axis worth flagging next to Arrow / Ge-Halpern / Black is mechanism design under truthful revelation — the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves family for cardinal aggregation in the alignment setting where annotators may strategically misreport to influence the trained policy. The standard social-choice frame analyses what aggregation rules can satisfy; the VCG frame analyses what incentives induce honest preference reports. Bowling-Brown discussions of honest preference elicitation hint at this direction; a systematic VCG-RLHF treatment is open.

    7.9 The Black 1948 escape: single-peaked preferences#

    A frequently-overlooked escape from Arrow-type impossibilities is single-peaked preferences (Black 1948; Sen 1966). If voters have single-peaked preferences over a one-dimensional alternative space — each voter has a single bliss point with monotonically decreasing preference as alternatives move away in either direction — pairwise majority voting yields a complete transitive social order whose top element is the bliss point of the median voter. The escape works by violating Arrow's U-domain axiom (the social welfare function is defined only on single-peaked profiles, not on every logically possible profile). Sen (1966) generalised to value-restricted preferences: any restriction excluding Condorcet-cycle patterns yields transitive majority rule.

    The implication for RLHF is empirical. If human annotators' preferences over LLM completions are effectively single-peaked along some latent quality dimension — for instance, a one-dimensional helpfulness-safety trade-off axis with each annotator's bliss point being their personal preferred trade-off — median aggregation would suffice and Arrow-type impossibilities are not the binding constraint. Whether this empirical condition holds is part of what Ge-Halpern's framework is designed to make precise; current RLHF practice does not directly test it.

    7.10 Constitutional AI as soft lexicographic ordering#

    Bai, Kadavath, Kundu, Askell, Kernion, Jones, et al. (2022) — fifty-one authors at Anthropic — introduced Constitutional AI as a two-phase fine-tuning procedure. The first phase (SL-CAI) is supervised constitutional revision: the model rewrites its own outputs against a list of constitutional principles, then SFT-trains on the revisions. The second phase (RL-CAI) is RLAIF (RL from AI Feedback): use AI-generated preference labels to train a preference model, then RL.

    A common misframing in informal alignment writing describes Constitutional AI as enforcing "non-compensatory" preferences in the MOO sense, where safety principles would dominate helpfulness lexicographically. This is not what the implementation does. Both SL-CAI and RL-CAI are differentiable; gradient updates trade off principles smoothly, and the SFT and RLAIF losses are fully compensatory (improvements on one principle can offset losses on another in the gradient signal). The "constitutional" non-compensatory aspirations live in the principle list, where principles are phrased absolutely ("never produce content that …"). The implementation enforces them only as soft preferences in the supervised and preference-model data — i.e., as soft lexicographic ordering achieved by iterative refinement rather than hard non-compensatory filtering.

    The distinction matters operationally. Hard non-compensatory filtering would require a separate safety classifier acting as a hard filter on outputs — not a fine-tuning target. CAI does not implement this. The recurring informal claim that CAI enforces "absolute" principles via fine-tuning conflates the principle list (absolute language) with the loss function (compensatory) [†caveat-CAI].

    A second signpost. The social-choice triple (§7.7-§7.9) and CAI (§7.10) addressed whose preferences and how aggregated. The remaining three subsections (§7.11-§7.13) address the regularisation choice as hidden MOO — Thread 2 here resurfaces in operational form. \(f\)-DPO recasts the divergence choice as scalarising fidelity-vs-coverage; the DRO equivalence makes the KL temperature \(\beta\) legible as a robustness-budget radius; the Goodhart distinction names the failure mode that this whole apparatus is trying to push back against.

    7.11 \(f\)-DPO and the hidden MOO over fidelity-vs-diversity#

    Wang, Jiang, Yang, Liu, Chen (2024, ICLR) generalised DPO's reverse-KL regulariser to any \(f\)-divergence: \(D_f(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}[f(\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})]\). For Jensen-Shannon, forward-KL, and \(\alpha\)-divergences, the KKT analysis still yields a closed-form implicit reward — the key technical contribution; without it the DPO substitution trick fails. Practical finding: forward-KL is mode-covering (broader, more diverse outputs at the cost of safety guarantees), reverse-KL is mode-seeking (sharper outputs but riskier), JS sits in between. A separate line — Go, Korbak, Kruszewski et al. (ICML 2023), "Aligning Language Models with Preferences through f-divergence Minimization" — establishes the same finding in the original (non-DPO) RLHF pipeline.

    The survey-level claim that \(f\)-divergence selection is equivalent to scalarisation over (fidelity, mode-coverage) is the sharpest articulation of "RLHF regularisation choice is hidden MOO." It is supported empirically by both Wang et al. (2024) for DPO and Go et al. (2023) for PPO-based RLHF.

    7.12 DRO and KL-RLHF: the same identity from two angles#

    Distributionally robust optimisation (DRO) seeks a decision that performs well not against a single distribution but against the worst case within a ball of distributions around a nominal one. The ball's geometry (KL, Wasserstein, \(\chi^2\), moment constraints, …) determines what kind of robustness guarantee one buys. The standard KL-regularised RLHF objective turns out to be exactly the DRO Lagrangian for a KL ball around \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Concretely, \(\max_\pi\mathbb{E}_\pi[R] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\) is the Lagrangian relaxation of a KL-ball distributionally robust optimisation problem: $$ \max_\pi\mathbb{E}\pi[R] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,|\,\pi}}) \equiv \max_\pi\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{B{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi $$ where }})}\mathbb{E}_Q[R], \tag{E26\(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \{Q:\mathrm{KL}(Q\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\le \rho\}\). The strong-duality bridge (Ben-Tal et al. 2013; Esfahani and Kuhn 2018 for Wasserstein) connects \(\beta\) and \(\rho\) monotonically. An operational consequence: practitioners tune \(\beta\) as a "KL penalty weight," but \(\rho\) is interpretable as a robustness budget — the maximum distributional shift the policy protects against — and alignment reporting might be more honest if it disclosed \(\rho\) alongside or instead of \(\beta\). Choosing the divergence in DPO or RLHF is choosing the DRO ambiguity geometry: - KL ball \(\Rightarrow\) standard RLHF / DPO. - Wasserstein-\(p\) ball \(\Rightarrow\) Wasserstein-DPO (WDPO; Wu et al. 2024-2025). - \(\chi^2\) ball \(\Rightarrow\) CVaR-style worst-case. - Moment constraints \(\Rightarrow\) method-of-moments DRO.

    The 2024-2025 literature has now operationalised this identity. The DRO-REBEL paper (arXiv:2509.19104, 2025) gives explicit sample-complexity bounds for Wasserstein-DPO; Hu and Hong's DRRO line shows that worst-case regret is less pessimistic than worst-case value, making it a more practical formulation. The systematic mapping of \(f\)-divergence choice to DRO ambiguity geometry is recent and not yet textbook material [†caveat-DRO], but it is now concrete enough that a survey can cite it as established rather than future work.

    7.13 Goodhart, not Lucas: the right name for RLHF reward over-optimisation#

    The one-line distinction: Lucas = your model re-optimises when policy changes. Goodhart = your proxy breaks when you push it too far. RLHF freezes the reward model; the reward model does not re-optimise. So RLHF over-optimisation is Goodhart, not Lucas.

    A recurring misattribution in alignment writing is to call RLHF reward over-optimisation an instance of "the Lucas critique." The Lucas critique (Lucas 1976) states that behavioural parameters of a macroeconomic model are not policy-invariant: agents re-optimise in response to anticipated policy changes, so the model itself mis-specifies once policy moves. The critique is about expectations and the rational re-optimisation of the model's own parameters.

    In RLHF the reward model \(R_\phi\) is frozen during PPO or DPO training. Its parameters do not respond to the policy's optimisation. The policy \(\pi_\theta\) shifts the input distribution of \((x,y)\) into regions where \(R_\phi\) generalises poorly. Distribution shift plus frozen proxy is the classical Goodhart's Law setup, not the Lucas critique. Lucas would require the reward model itself to update in response to policy changes — which is precisely what online reward modelling (RLHF with on-policy labels, or the iterative CAI loop) does, and which actually helps by closing the proxy-true gap.

    Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) catalogue four variants of Goodhart: 1. Regressional Goodhart: selecting for proxy \(\hat{r}\) also selects for noise \(\hat{r} - r^\star\). 2. Extremal Goodhart: at the optimisation's edge, proxy-true correlation breaks down because proxy fits in-distribution but does not extrapolate. 3. Causal Goodhart: proxy correlates with true via a common cause; intervening on proxy fails to move true. 4. Adversarial Goodhart: optimisation creates incentive for adversaries to game the proxy.

    RLHF reward over-optimisation is most cleanly extremal Goodhart. The empirical anchor is Gao, Schulman, Hilton (2023), "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization," whose central curve traces the proxy-true reward gap as a function of KL distance from the reference policy and identifies the regime where proxy reward continues to climb while true reward starts to fall.

    The Goodhart-vs-Lucas distinction is substantive and prescriptive: Lucas-type problems are addressed by modelling expectations; Goodhart-type problems are addressed by closing the proxy-true gap (online reward modelling, iterative refinement, or robust ambiguity sets via DRO). The "Lucas critique" framing for RLHF reward over-optimisation that occasionally appears in alignment writing is strictly incorrect; the right name is "Goodhart's Law (extremal variant)" [†caveat-Lucas].

    §8 Lateral Connections: Three Threads Consolidated#

    Three threads — welfare economics, information theory, Goodhart's Law — run through §2, §5, §7; this section names them as threads, gives each its anchor reference, and explicitly states where each thread is established literature versus informal analogy.

    8.1 Thread 1: Welfare economics#

    Four correspondences sit on this thread.

    Utilitarian welfare ↔ weighted sum. Weighted-sum scalarisation \(\min_x \sum_i w_i f_i\) is the classical utilitarian aggregation: total welfare is the weighted sum of individual welfares. The mathematical equivalence is exact and established in Ehrgott (2005).

    Rawlsian max-min ↔ Tchebycheff. The Rawlsian principle of maximising the welfare of the worst-off agent corresponds to the Tchebycheff scalarisation \(\min_x\max_i w_i|f_i - z_i^\star|\). The equivalence is structural: both pick the point that minimises the worst weighted deviation from the ideal.

    Nash bargaining ↔ Nash-MTL. The Nash 1950 bargaining solution and its four axioms are imported wholesale into Nash-MTL (Navon et al. 2022); the disagreement-point choice is the additional normative parameter that bargaining theory makes explicit but Nash-MTL implementations tend to elide.

    Sen capability ↔ hypervolume. Sen's (1985) capability approach describes well-being as a vector of functionings \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^d_{\ge 0}\) (nutrition, mobility, literacy, …). Each person has a capability set \(Q_i\), the set of functioning vectors they can achieve. The dominance order is, in Sen's words (1985 §5): "if person \(i\) has more of a significant functioning than person \(j\), and at least as much of all such functionings, then \(i\) clearly has a higher valued functioning vector than \(j\)" — literally the Pareto dominance order on functionings.

    A natural welfare measure on capability sets would be the hypervolume of the achievable functioning region (with a reference point of basic-functioning floor). The correspondence is suggestive enough that one might expect a published formalisation. Five searches combining {Sen, capability, hypervolume, vector-valued, Pareto} returned no paper that draws this correspondence. The closest formal works are Alkire and Foster (2011) on multidimensional poverty indices and Bourguignon and Chakravarty (2003) on axiomatic multidimensional welfare; neither uses hypervolume. The Sen-hypervolume correspondence is therefore an informal cross-domain analogy at present, not literature [†caveat-S]. It remains a clean opportunity for either welfare economists or EMO researchers to formalise.

    8.2 Thread 2: Information theory and \(f\)-divergence as hidden MOO#

    The \(f\)-divergence framework provides the sharpest information-theoretic interpretation of MOO embedded in modern alignment. Each \(f\)-divergence implements a different scalarisation of (fidelity-to-preference, mode-coverage-of-data). Forward-KL favours mode-coverage; reverse-KL favours mode-seeking; Jensen-Shannon sits between. The mode-seeking/mode-covering distinction is from Wang, Jiang, Yang, Liu, Chen (2024, ICLR).

    The identity "\(f\)-divergence selection = scalarisation of (fidelity, mode-coverage)" is supported on two fronts: Wang et al. (2024) for the DPO pipeline (closed-form implicit reward for non-KL \(f\)); Go, Korbak, Kruszewski et al. (2023, ICML) for the standard (non-DPO) RLHF pipeline. Within Thread 2 the further identity — KL-RLHF as KL-ball DRO (E26) — is established via Ben-Tal et al. (2013), Esfahani and Kuhn (2018), and is now operationalised by WDPO, DRRO, and DRO-REBEL (2024-2025). The Thread 2 claim "regularisation choice is hidden MOO" is therefore on firm ground in 2025, even though the systematic mapping is recent.

    8.3 Thread 3: Goodhart's Law (not Lucas critique)#

    The Goodhart-vs-Lucas distinction (§7.13) is the most consequential terminological correction this survey makes. Lucas requires that the model re-optimise in response to policy. RLHF has a frozen reward model, hence Goodhart. The empirical anchor is Gao, Schulman, Hilton (2023). The taxonomic anchor is Manheim and Garrabrant (2018).

    Two further connections sit on Thread 3. The first connects back to §6: in MORL, the analogous failure mode is value-function over-optimisation in offline RL, where the value function plays the role RLHF's reward model plays. The second points forward to §10's open problems: online reward modelling — where the reward model updates in response to policy distribution shift — converts Extremal Goodhart into the (much weaker) Regressional Goodhart variant, since the proxy is no longer frozen. This is the structural reason that iterative CAI and online DPO empirically resist some forms of reward hacking that standard RLHF cannot.

    8.4 Thread 4: Order theory and the cone framing#

    Thread 4 is the cone-induced partial order. It reappears in §2 for Pareto / weak / strict / proper efficiency, in §5 for the (informal) "common descent cone" framing, in §6 for the vector-Bellman partial order over \(\mathbb{R}^k\), and in §7 for the Bradley-Terry violation of Arrow's total-order assumption. The cone formalism (Sawaragi, Nakayama, Tanino 1985) is established literature. The "common descent cone" of §5 is not literature — Fliege and Svaiter (2000) speak of the "common descent direction set," Désidéri (2012) speaks of the "convex hull of gradients," and the recent gradient-MOO survey (Lin et al. 2025) speaks of "directional constraints on the update vector." The phrase "common descent cone" in this survey is pedagogical synthesis [†caveat-C].

    8.5 Thread 5: Decomposition as a design pattern across communities#

    The MOEA/D design pattern — solve \(N\) scalar subproblems with niche-mediated cooperation, one per preference weight vector — is the conceptual ancestor of three later constructions in different communities. Pareto Hypernetworks (Navon et al. 2021) map a preference vector to model parameters via a hypernetwork — the same preference-indexed structure, with hypernet inference replacing population maintenance. Pareto Set Learning (Lin et al. 2020 for MTL; Lin et al. 2022 for expensive MOO) maps a preference vector to decision variables, with the same indexing logic. Rewarded Soups (Rame et al. 2023) preserves the preference-indexed structure via linear interpolation of fine-tuned model weights, with the structural simplification that the \(N\) "subproblems" are the \(k\) single-reward fine-tunes and the preference index is a single \(\lambda\in\Delta^{k-1}\).

    The anchor sentence from Panacea (Zhong et al. 2024) — "Instead of just learning one such solution, we focus on learning the entire set of Pareto-optimal solutions" — is the sharpest articulation of the design pattern across MOEA/D, PHN, PSL, Rewarded Soups, Panacea, and MO-ODPO. The intellectual-lineage claim from MOEA/D to PHN/Soups is supportable on technical grounds but is rarely cited by the deep-learning side; we make the connection explicit while acknowledging that it is the surveyor's pedagogical observation rather than a lineage already documented in the ML literature.

    8.6 Cross-thread interactions#

    Two cross-thread interactions are worth flagging because they recur in the literature.

    Thread 1 × Thread 3. The Nash bargaining solution (Thread 1) presumes a frozen disagreement point; Goodhart over-optimisation (Thread 3) presumes a frozen proxy. Both pathologies share the structure "an algorithm optimises with respect to a fixed reference object that the optimisation itself stresses." Nash-MTL's update direction is fair by construction given its disagreement point; RLHF's policy is reward-maximising given its reward model. In both cases, periodically re-anchoring the reference object — disagreement point set by the current single-task optima; reward model updated on on-policy samples — closes the loop and changes the failure mode qualitatively. This is the structural reason iterative CAI and online DPO differ in failure regime from one-shot RLHF.

    Thread 2 × Thread 4. The \(f\)-divergence regularisation choice (Thread 2) and the dominance-cone choice (Thread 4) are linked at the level of preference geometry: a tilted cone \(D'\) shifting Pareto dominance in a specific direction has, on the policy-output space, the same effect as a specific \(f\)-divergence (asymmetric KL, \(\alpha\)-divergence, …) shifting the regularisation in a corresponding direction. The Amari-Nagaoka dual-flat-affine connection on the \(f\)-divergence side has a known formal link to dually-flat manifolds, and the bridge to dominance cones runs through Csiszár's information geometry for the static case; for the RLHF setting the cone-divergence correspondence is folklore at present, not yet textbook [†caveat-Info-Geom].

    Cross-thread interactions are part of why MOO recurs across communities: addressing one thread engages another. A reader of the alignment literature who encounters a debate about KL versus JS divergence is implicitly engaged in a Thread 2 question; a reader of the gradient-MTL literature debating disagreement-point choice for Nash-MTL is implicitly engaged in a Thread 1 question. Naming the threads gives the cross-community reader vocabulary to recognise the same question recurring in a new disguise.

    §9 Critical Reading: Developing Taste in a Chaotic Field#

    Four (and a half) case studies in reading MOO papers critically.

    9.1 Pareto-stationarity counter-examples on the simplex boundary#

    A standard heuristic in MTL papers is to display a Pareto-stationarity certificate \(\sum_i\alpha_i\nabla J_i = 0\) with a degenerate case — say \(\alpha_1 = 1, \alpha_2 = 0\) — as the worked example. The example is degenerate because it collapses to single-objective stationarity of \(J_1\), contributing nothing to the multi-objective story. The critical-reading lesson is that any displayed counter-example with \(\alpha_i \in \{0,1\}\) is on the simplex boundary, and boundary examples do not exercise the interior structure that makes Pareto-stationarity informative. The genuine non-degenerate construction is Désidéri (2014)'s MGDA-II / Gram-Schmidt construction in (E5), where every \(\alpha_i\in(0,1)\) and the directional derivative \(\langle u_i, \mathbf{d}^\star\rangle\) is equal across all objectives. When reading a Pareto-stationarity proof, check that the worked example is interior.

    9.2 \(\Delta m\%\) as a sole metric vs. HV / IGD\(^+\)#

    The MTL community's default reporting metric, \(\Delta m\%\) — the average percentage change in benchmark task metrics relative to single-task baselines — has two structural problems. First, it conflates distance-to-front (how good the model's Pareto trade-off is) with coverage (which trade-offs the model can express); these are separate things in EMO indicator theory and are separated by HV (which measures dominated volume) and IGD\(^+\) (which measures average distance to a reference Pareto set). Second, it is sensitive to baseline choice — a strong single-task baseline reduces room for negative \(\Delta m\%\). Third, it has high seed variance: FAMO's NYUv2 number (\(-0.13\%\) main table, \(-0.27\%\) at the unfavourable end of the seed range; ConsMTL's \(-0.59\%\) on NYUv2, with \(-1.42\%\) appearing on a different benchmark in the same paper) shows variance that is not always disclosed.

    The EMO community's standard practice — HV for \(M\le 4\), IGD\(^+\) for \(M\ge 5\), \(\ge 31\) independent trials, Wilcoxon rank-sum at \(\alpha=0.05\) with Bonferroni correction — would substantially raise the bar for MTL reporting. Adoption is straightforward: HV requires only a reference point disclosed; IGD\(^+\) requires a reference Pareto set, sampled or estimated from a careful baseline grid. The implementation cost is single-digit hours on standard MTL benchmarks.

    9.3 Gradient-surgery claims that don't survive \(k\ge 3\) tasks#

    PCGrad's empirical effectiveness on \(k=3\) MTL benchmarks (NYUv2, CityScapes) is real. Its theoretical guarantee is only proved for the \(k=2\) convex case. The random-task-order in Algorithm 1 of Yu et al. (2020) is itself an admission that order matters at \(k\ge 3\). Liu et al.'s CAGrad paper (Section 2.2) notes that PCGrad does not always converge to a Pareto-stationary point at \(k\ge 3\). The "alternating projections / POCS" analogy is the surveyor's framing of what could fail, but no published paper has produced a rigorous cycling counter-example.

    The critical-reading lesson: distinguish empirical effectiveness on benchmark \(k\) from theoretical guarantees over arbitrary \(k\). When a paper proves a \(k=2\) convergence theorem and reports \(k=3\) benchmark numbers, the gap between proof and experiment is doing the work, and the gap is rarely interrogated [†caveat-P].

    9.4 RLHF reward-hacking misattributions#

    The recurring misattribution "RLHF reward over-optimisation is the Lucas critique" is wrong. The Lucas critique requires the model to re-optimise; RLHF has a frozen reward model. The phenomenon is Goodhart's Law (extremal variant) per Manheim and Garrabrant (2018), with the Gao-Schulman-Hilton (2023) scaling curve as the empirical anchor. The misattribution matters because the prescription differs: Lucas-type problems are addressed by modelling expectations, while Goodhart-type problems are addressed by closing the proxy-true gap (online reward modelling, iterative refinement, or robust ambiguity sets via DRO).

    Parallel misattributions: - "RLHF violates Arrow's theorem" — wrong. RLHF operates outside Arrow's scope by using cardinal Bradley-Terry scores. The correct impossibility is Ge-Halpern (2024) in the linear social-choice framework. - "Constitutional AI implements hard non-compensatory preferences" — wrong. SL-CAI and RL-CAI are differentiable; the implementation is fully compensatory. CAI is soft lexicographic ordering via iterative refinement, not hard filtering.

    The general lesson: terminology that sounds technically sophisticated (Lucas, Arrow, non-compensatory) attracts citations even when its premises do not hold in the cited setting. Critical reading requires checking the technical preconditions of every cited theorem.

    9.5 Informal vs. formal cross-domain bridges: the Tchebycheff-tropical case#

    A teachable case in distinguishing formal from informal cross-domain bridges is the Tchebycheff-tropical analogy. Tropical geometry (max-plus or min-plus semiring) is the natural algebraic language for piecewise-linear functions; the Tchebycheff scalarisation is structurally a max-plus tropical polynomial. The bridge is suggestive, the language is right, and the combinatorial tools of tropical polytopes (Joswig 2022) would in principle apply to piecewise-linear Pareto front analysis. But no peer-reviewed paper has developed the identification as a stand-alone theorem. The Tchebycheff-tropical identity is informal cross-domain analogy at present.

    The distinction between informal-but-suggestive and formal-and-cited is itself a critical-reading skill. We explicitly label the Tchebycheff-tropical analogy as informal [†caveat-T], the Sen-hypervolume correspondence as informal [†caveat-S], and the "common descent cone" framing as surveyor synthesis [†caveat-C]. A reader who internalises these distinctions will spot the same pattern in other corners of the literature where evocative names are not yet backed by theorems.

    9.6 Author lists and venue claims: the small things that matter#

    Fact-checking author lists and venues is tedious and indispensable. Secondary surveys in this field frequently mis-render author lists and venues: Nash-MTL is a seven-author paper (Navon, Shamsian, Achituve, Maron, Kawaguchi, Chechik, Fetaya), but appears in citations with six names in scrambled order; PCGrad has six authors with Finn as the last, sometimes cited with Finn omitted; CAGrad's five-author list (B. Liu, X. Liu, X. Jin, P. Stone, Q. Liu) gets replaced in secondary citations with fictitious names; GradDrop's correct seventh author is Y. Chai, occasionally rendered as "Tan." Venue claims drift similarly: FAMO is NeurIPS 2023, not 2024; MODPO is Findings of ACL 2024, not the main conference; Rewarded Soups is NeurIPS 2023, not ICML 2024; Safe RLHF's middle names are Ji, Xu, Liu (Jiaming Ji, Xinbo Xu, Mickel Liu).

    The critical-reading discipline is to cross-check author lists and venues against the official proceedings record (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR, ACL Anthology, etc.) for every paper that a survey cites with non-trivial weight. The cost is single-digit minutes per paper; the benefit is bibliographic correctness and a credit trail that does not silently propagate errors into the next generation of surveys.

    9.7 Reading numbers across benchmarks#

    A subtler failure mode is cross-benchmark contamination of headline numbers. ConsMTL's \(-0.59\%\) NYUv2 figure and its \(-1.42\%\) figure on (likely) CityScapes appear in the same paper; secondary surveys occasionally attach the \(-1.42\%\) figure to NYUv2. FAMO's MTAN-NYUv2 headline \(-0.13\%\) versus the unfavourable-seed \(-0.27\%\) is a within-paper variance issue.

    The critical-reading discipline is twofold: when citing a single number, name the (paper, benchmark, backbone, seed budget) tuple explicitly; when surveying across papers, check that the numbers cited for one benchmark were actually reported on that benchmark. The cost is low; the survey-quality benefit is large because cross-benchmark contamination silently propagates between surveys.

    §10 Open Problems and Research Frontier#

    Five open problems, each tied to a deep-research-identified gap.

    (i) MTL adoption of HV / IGD\(^+\) as standard reporting. The EMO community has 25 years of indicator theory: strict Pareto-monotonicity of HV, weak Pareto-compliance of IGD\(^+\), the slope-bias of HV optimal \(\mu\)-distributions, the #P-hardness of exact HV in \(d\) dimensions. The MTL community defaults to \(\Delta m\%\), which is neither Pareto-monotonic nor coverage-aware. Adopting HV (for \(M\le 4\)) and IGD\(^+\) (for \(M\ge 5\)) as standard secondary indicators is operationally straightforward and would raise the rigour of MTL benchmarking substantially. The remaining theoretical task is to characterise indicator behaviour in neural-network outcome space, where Pareto fronts are not analytic curves but are induced by training dynamics — an underdeveloped corner of EMO indicator theory.

    (ii) Formal cycling theorems for PCGrad at \(k\ge 3\). No published paper has produced a rigorous PCGrad cycling counter-example at \(k\ge 3\). Liu et al.'s CAGrad (2021) observes the gap; the alternating-projections analogy is suggestive but informal. A clean construction would settle whether the gap is intrinsic or artefactual, and would clarify whether projection-based gradient surgery is fundamentally limited to \(k=2\) or whether stronger structural assumptions could rescue the \(k\ge 3\) guarantee.

    (iii) Zero-duality-gap bounds for RLHF-scale function approximation. Safe RLHF implicitly relies on the Paternain et al. (2019) result that zero duality gap holds for sufficiently rich policy classes. The "sufficiently rich" condition is qualitative; quantitative statements for transformer-scale policies — what richness measure, what tolerance — are not in the literature. A first quantitative version would substantially harden the theoretical claim that Safe RLHF's primal-dual updates converge.

    (iv) Wasserstein-DPO sample complexity in the high-dimensional preference regime. WDPO has explicit sample-complexity bounds for low-dimensional preference embeddings (DRO-REBEL 2025). High-dimensional preference geometries — where preferences are themselves embedded by a learned model — change the Wasserstein metric's behaviour. Sample complexity in this regime is open.

    (v) Preference-conditioned DPO with non-linear utility functions. MODPO assumes rewards decompose linearly across \(k\) objectives; the assumption is operationally convenient but rules out concave utilities (saturating helpfulness) and lexicographic safety regimes. A non-linear extension — where the implicit-reward identity (E23) is generalised to a non-linear scalarisation \(u(r_1,\dots,r_k)\) — is a clean theoretical target with significant downstream consequences for alignment.

    Three further open problems are worth flagging though not numbered. (a) The Sen-hypervolume formalisation [†caveat-S] is a single-paper opportunity bridging welfare economics and EMO indicator theory. (b) The Tchebycheff-tropical identification [†caveat-T] is a single-paper opportunity bridging tropical geometry and piecewise-linear Pareto front analysis. (c) The MOEA/D-to-PHN intellectual lineage is well-supported on technical grounds and would benefit from explicit historical-attribution work in the deep-learning literature.

    Four further methodological gaps that surface across the five communities are worth listing because their resolution is mostly empirical rather than theoretical. (d) MTL benchmarks at \(M\ge 4\) are scarce; constructing standard high-\(M\) MTL benchmarks would let the EMO indicators discriminate among MTL methods more meaningfully. (e) RLHF benchmark suites do not standardly report Pareto-coverage indicators; preference-conditioned methods are evaluated on a small grid of \(w\) values, and the survey-wide adoption of HV / IGD\(^+\) on preference simplices is a near-term opportunity. (f) The interaction between online reward modelling and Goodhart over-optimisation has been studied empirically (Christiano et al., Stiennon et al., iterative CAI) but not theoretically; a clean theoretical separation of online from offline reward-modelling regimes in the Manheim-Garrabrant taxonomy would be valuable. (g) The connection between Pareto-stationarity in deep MTL and generalisation (not just empirical optimisation) is partially developed by the Three-Way Trade-off result; extending it to non-MGDA methods (PCGrad, Nash-MTL, FAMO) is an open direction.

    Three deeper why-questions surfaced during the survey's development and remain open. (h) Is there a single meta-axis behind §1's three loosening lines — for instance, a shrinking epistemic commitment to the scalarisation map from globally trusted \(w\) through locally trusted anchor to gradient-only trust to runtime-input trust? The "three parallel" framing is descriptively accurate but does not preclude such an axis. (i) Why Nash bargaining as the cooperative-game template for Nash-MTL (§5.4) rather than Kalai-Smorodinsky or Egalitarian (Kalai 1977)? Each induces a different update direction with its own normative content; the current choice is historical. (j) Why does Rewarded Soups' linear interpolation succeed (§7.4) when MOEA/D explicitly rejects linear combination for non-convex fronts (§4.2)? Either Soups recovers only the convex-supported portion of the alignment front, or linear mode connectivity makes the loss landscape implicitly convex along the interpolation segment. The survey notes the tension but does not resolve it.

    10.1 Looking back at the §1 thesis#

    The §1 thesis was that MOO has three or four parallel loosening lines rather than a single technical thread of the kind bilevel optimisation organises around. Six chapters later, the parallelism is concretely visible: the convex-front assumption (§2.4-§2.6, §4.2, §5.3, §7.4) is loosened by Tchebycheff, population search, MGDA's min-norm element, and weight interpolation — four communities, four different mechanisms. The static-weight assumption is loosened by adaptive weighting, computed task weights, hypernet conditioning, and inference-time interpolation. The single-solution-output assumption is loosened from compromise point to approximating set to preference-indexed family. The threading claim — that welfare economics, \(f\)-divergence, Goodhart, order theory, and decomposition recur laterally across §2-§7 — is borne out: each thread surfaces in at least three sections (§8 audits this).

    Three boundary conditions on this thesis. (CA1) The survey is not field-unifying in the strongest sense: there is no transfer theorem here, no result proved in one community whose conclusion holds with the same proof in another. The lateral threads are translation correspondences. A reader who maintains "five distinct fields with incidental Pareto vocabulary" can hold that position consistently; the survey demonstrates that vocabularies can be aligned, not that fields are equivalent under alignment. (CA2) In overparameterised deep MTL, every interpolating solution is trivially Pareto-stationary; §5's machinery describes a condition that holds at convergence, and the operationally interesting object is the trajectory-implicit selection among Pareto-stationary minimisers (Soudry et al. 2018). §5 should be read as describing under-parameterised intuition that survives partially into the overparameterised regime. (CA3) Preference-conditioned methods at deployment usually have the system designer, not the end-user, supplying \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) — behavioural-economics evidence (Tversky-Kahneman; Lichtenstein-Slovic) suggests humans cannot stably report cardinal trade-offs at \(k\ge 4\). The preference simplex is a design freedom for admin dashboards and A/B testing; the gap with the preference-elicitation literature (Chajewska-Koller-Parr 2000; Boutilier) remains for future work.

    What survives: four (or five) communities have independently re-derived a common toolkit under disjoint vocabularies, the parallelism is structural rather than coincidental, and the cross-community reader benefits more from a translation table and a thread-naming exercise than from a forced single-axis unification.

    11.1 Modern On-Ramps#

    Six sources for a reader who wants confident orientation after this survey, chosen so that the union covers the gradient-MTL, MORL, RLHF, and EMO entry points without duplication.

    • Lin, Yang, Hu, Yan, Zhang, Zhao, Kwok (2025), Gradient-Based Multi-Objective Deep Learning: Algorithms, Theories, Applications, and Beyond. arXiv:2501.10945. — The right next read for a deep-MTL reader. Taxonomises gradient-based MOO by output type (single solution / finite set / continuous Pareto set), which exposes structural similarities the per-method literature obscures; this is the lens §5.10–§5.11 use to place ConsMTL alongside PHN and PSL. Read §1–§4 first; the application sections can be skimmed.
    • Hayes, Rǎdulescu, Bargiacchi, Källström, Macfarlane, Reymond, Verstraeten, Zintgraf, Dazeley, Heintz, Howley, Irissappane, Mannion, Nowé, Ramos, Restelli, Vamplew, Roijers (2022), A Practical Guide to Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning and Planning, Autonomous Agents and Multi-Agent Systems 36:26. — The reference §6 leans on. The 59-page journal version (not the AAMAS brief) develops the SER/ESR distinction, the deterministic-vs-stochastic-policy layer, and the utility-based taxonomy that §6.3–§6.4 inherit. Pair with MORL-Baselines (NeurIPS 2023) and MO-Gymnasium to ground the formalism in code.
    • Lambert (2025), Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (rlhfbook.com, online; print edition forthcoming, Manning). — Practitioner-level companion to §7. The over-optimisation chapter is the most honest written treatment of the phenomenon §7.13 names as extremal Goodhart, even though Lambert does not adopt that terminology. The Constitutional AI chapter is the right complement to §7.10's soft-lexicographic framing.
    • Ehrgott, Köksalan, Kadziński, Deb (2025), Fifty Years of Multi-Objective Optimization and Decision-Making: From Mathematical Programming to Evolutionary Computation, European J. Operational Research, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2025.06.012. — Anchors the OR foundations a deep-learning reader rarely sees. Maps fragmentation and convergence across MCDM, EMO, and preference modelling over five decades; the discussion of interactive methods is the entry point a reader needs after recognising that §1's static-weight loosening line maps to OR's interactive-methods quadrant.
    • Miettinen (1999), Nonlinear Multiobjective Optimization. Kluwer / Springer. — Where the scalarisation theory of §2.4 actually lives. Chapters 2–4 give the supporting-hyperplane argument behind the convex-front limit that §1's loosening thread (i) opens with; Chapter 5 covers the interactive-methods quadrant a deep-learning reader will have never encountered. The most efficient route to understanding why linear scalarisation cannot reach concave Pareto regions, not just that it cannot.
    • Ehrgott (2005), Multicriteria Optimization, 2nd ed. Springer. — The textbook §2's order-theoretic core leans on. Cone-dominance, proper efficiency, and the simplex-dimension convention used throughout §0 are all set here at textbook pace. Use as a reference rather than reading cover-to-cover.

    11.2 Enduring Ideas#

    Six priors the ML literature keeps re-deriving. A reader who recognises the originals will spot new instances of the same construction in current papers.

    • Sawaragi, Nakayama, Tanino (1985), Theory of Multiobjective Optimization, Academic Press, Mathematics in Science and Engineering 176. — The axiomatic foundation §2.1 cites. Introduces the closed pointed convex domination cone \(D\), the cone-induced partial order, and the existence/scalarisation/duality propositions that recur in every later MOO textbook. The phrase "common descent cone" used informally in §5 [†caveat-C] is pedagogical, not theirs.
    • Désidéri (2012), Multiple-Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA) for Multiobjective Optimization, Comptes Rendus Mathématique 350(5–6):313–318; and Désidéri (2014), follow-up MGDA-II/III chapter in Modeling, Simulation and Optimization for Science and Technology, Springer. — The min-norm-element construction at the heart of §5.1, and the Gram-Schmidt non-degenerate construction §9.1 uses to call out boundary-degeneracy in MTL displayed examples. Read together; the 2012 paper sets the QP, the 2014 chapter handles the geometry.
    • Steuer (1986), Multiple Criteria Optimization: Theory, Computation, and Application, Wiley. — The first textbook statement that weighted Tchebycheff scalarisation reaches every properly-Pareto point, including the concave regions weighted-sum cannot. §2.4 and §4.2 both descend from this result; recognising it as a 1986 fact makes the post-2020 ML rediscoveries legible as rediscoveries.
    • Fliege and Svaiter (2000), Steepest Descent Methods for Multicriteria Optimization, Math. Methods of Operations Research 51:479–494. — Predates MGDA in spirit and supplies the "common descent direction" vocabulary that Désidéri later sharpens. The right reference for a reader who wants to see that the §5 gradient construction did not start with deep learning.
    • Deb, Pratap, Agarwal, Meyarivan (2002) NSGA-II, IEEE T. Evolutionary Computation 6(2):182–197; Zhang and Li (2007) MOEA/D, IEEE T. Evolutionary Computation 11(6):712–731. — Read together. NSGA-II is the canonical Pareto-rank-plus-crowding algorithm; MOEA/D introduces the decomposition pattern that §8.5 traces forward into PHN, PSL, Rewarded Soups, Panacea, and MO-ODPO. The preference-indexed structure on the alignment side is MOEA/D's intellectual lineage, even when uncited.
    • Zitzler and Thiele (1999), Multiobjective Evolutionary Algorithms: a Comparative Case Study and the Strength Pareto Approach, IEEE T. Evolutionary Computation 3(4):257–271; with Zitzler, Thiele, Laumanns, Fonseca, da Fonseca (2003) for the strict-Pareto-monotonicity proof, IEEE T. EC 7(2):117–132. — Where the hypervolume indicator is defined and where its uniqueness as a strictly Pareto-monotonic unary indicator is established. The §9.2 case for porting HV into MTL reporting cannot be made without these two as the technical anchor.

    11.3 Live Research Edge (2023–2026)#

    Seven items naming the open fronts. Each annotation flags the specific trend the work participates in.

    • L. Chen, H. Fernando, Y. Ying, T. Chen (2023, NeurIPS; extended in JMLR 25, 2024), Three-Way Trade-Off in Multi-Objective Learning. — The theoretical result §5.9 is built around. Proves that the dynamic-weighting step size couples convergence to a conflict-avoidant direction, convergence to Pareto stationarity, and generalisation error; the three cannot be simultaneously minimised. Reframes the gradient-MTL literature: simple static weighting remains competitive because aggressive conflict avoidance compresses the effective step size below what generalisation tolerates. Read the JMLR version; §1–§3 and Theorem 3.1 are the load-bearing parts.
    • Qin, Wang, Yan (2025) ConsMTL, AAAI; and B. Liu, Y. Feng, P. Stone, Q. Liu (2023) FAMO, NeurIPS. — The current state of the scalability front in gradient MTL. FAMO is the \(O(1)\) method §5.6 develops; ConsMTL is the bi-level routing approach §5.10 uses to show how task-specific parameters can absorb conflict that gradient surgery cannot. Read FAMO first, then ConsMTL; the comparison sharpens what "adaptive" means in §9.2's metric critique.
    • Shi, Zhou, Yang, Zhou, Hu, Hu, Aboutalebi, Liu, Wu, Yan, Lu, Mei (2025) MO-ODPO, AAAI; and Z. Zhou, J. Liu, J. Shao, X. Yue, C. Yang, W. Ouyang, Y. Qiao (2024) MODPO, Findings of ACL. — Read as a two-paper arc on multi-objective DPO. MODPO trains an implicit collective reward model \(r_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \sum_i \omega_i r_i\) and recovers the full preference-indexed family by varying \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) (§7.2); MO-ODPO adds online data generation and prompt-conditional inference (§7.3), producing a single policy that responds to new \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) at deployment. Code: github.com/ZHZisZZ/modpo.
    • Rame, Couairon, Shukor, Dancette, Gaya, Soulier, Cord (2023) Rewarded Soups, NeurIPS; Zhong, Liu, Yang, Wang, Lin, Wang, Lin (2024) Panacea, arXiv:2402.02030. — The §1 single-solution-output loosening line at its 2023–2024 frontier. Rewarded Soups uses linear weight interpolation of single-reward fine-tunes (§7.4) and exploits linear mode connectivity; Panacea folds the preference-indexed family into a single conditional model (§5.11). The unresolved tension is §10's open problem (j): why does linear interpolation work in alignment when MOEA/D specifically rejects it for non-convex fronts?
    • Wang, Jiang, Yang, Liu, Chen (2024) \(f\)-DPO, ICLR; Go, Korbak, Kruszewski, Rae, Krause, Dymetman (2023) \(f\)-divergence pre-training–to–alignment generalisation, ICML. — The information-theory side of Thread 2 (§8.2). Wang et al. derive closed-form implicit rewards for non-KL \(f\)-divergences and characterise the mode-seeking/mode-covering trade-off; Go et al. cover the same identity for the standard (non-DPO) RLHF pipeline. Together they establish that divergence selection is hidden scalarisation between fidelity and coverage.
    • DRO-REBEL (2025), arXiv:2509.19104; Wu et al. (2024–2025) WDPO/KLDPO, OpenReview D19hc2XPeZ. — The distributionally-robust corner of RLHF design space §7.12 develops. DRO-REBEL gives explicit sample-complexity bounds for Wasserstein-DPO; WDPO/KLDPO formalise the connection between RLHF's KL term and DRO ambiguity sets via Ben-Tal et al. (2013) and Esfahani–Kuhn (2018) strong-duality. The "ball geometry choice = robustness profile choice" claim of §7.12 is now operational, not aspirational.
    • Ge, Halpern, Micha, Procaccia, Shapira, Vorobeychik, Wu (2024), Axioms for AI Alignment from Human Feedback, NeurIPS. arXiv:2405.14758. — Replaces the loose "RLHF violates Arrow" framing common in 2023 alignment writing with the precise impossibility §7.7–§7.8 develop: in the linear social-choice framework, Bradley–Terry aggregation fails Pareto optimality. The right citation for an alignment reader who wants social-choice rigour without leaving the cardinal-utility setting RLHF actually operates in.

    Pair with two open-source benchmark stacks: LibMTL (median-research-group/LibMTL) for 13 gradient strategies under a unified PyTorch API, and LibMOON (xzhang2523/libmoon, NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks) for 20+ methods with GPU acceleration. Run both on a toy problem before reading further on §5; empirical intuition is irreplaceable.

    11.4 Cross-Domain Cross-Pollination#

    Five anchors for the lateral threads of §8, with explicit honesty markers on the bridges that remain informal.

    • Sen (1985), Commodities and Capabilities, North-Holland (also in Collective Choice and Social Welfare: An Expanded Edition, Harvard, 2017). — The welfare-economics anchor for Thread 1 (§8.1). The capability-set dominance order Sen develops on functioning vectors \(\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^d_{\ge 0}\) is literally Pareto dominance. The proposed Sen-hypervolume correspondence between capability-set welfare and hypervolume is informal cross-domain analogy at present, not literature [†caveat-S]; the closest formal work is Alkire–Foster (2011) on multidimensional poverty indices, which uses a different aggregator. Reading Sen establishes that the bridge is suggestive and unbuilt.
    • Nash (1950), The Bargaining Problem, Econometrica 18:155–162; and Kalai (1977), Proportional Solutions to Bargaining Situations: Interpersonal Utility Comparisons, Econometrica 45:1623–1630. — The cooperative-game template that Nash-MTL (§5.4) imports. Nash 1950 gives the four axioms (Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance, IIA) and the unique product solution; Kalai 1977 develops the egalitarian alternative that §10 open-problem (i) asks why Nash-MTL did not adopt. Reading both makes the disagreement-point normative choice §5.4 flags concrete.
    • Csiszár (1967, 1991) on \(f\)-divergence and information geometry; Amari and Nagaoka (2000), Methods of Information Geometry, AMS Translations of Mathematical Monographs 191. — The information-geometry side of Thread 2 (§8.2 and §8.6). The dual-flat-affine structure on the \(f\)-divergence side has a known formal link to dually-flat manifolds; the bridge from divergence selection to dominance-cone tilting (Thread 2 × Thread 4) goes through Csiszár's framework in the static case. The bridge from cone tilting to a specific \(f\)-divergence on the policy-output space remains folklore for the RLHF setting [†caveat-Info-Geom], not yet textbook.
    • Manheim and Garrabrant (2018), Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law, arXiv:1803.04585; and Gao, Schulman, Hilton (2023), Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization, arXiv:2210.10760. — Thread 3 (§8.3) reads. Manheim–Garrabrant supplies the four-variant taxonomy (regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial) that §7.13 uses to name RLHF over-optimisation as extremal Goodhart; Gao–Schulman–Hilton supplies the empirical scaling curve. Together they replace the Lucas-critique misattribution common in 2022–2023 alignment writing [†caveat-Lucas].
    • Maclagan and Sturmfels (2015), Introduction to Tropical Geometry, AMS Graduate Studies in Mathematics 161; and Mikhalkin (2005), Enumerative Tropical Algebraic Geometry in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), J. Amer. Math. Soc. 18(2):313–377. — Background for the Tchebycheff–tropical bridge of §4.7. Maclagan–Sturmfels gives the min-plus semiring and tropical polynomials; Mikhalkin develops the tropical-curve combinatorics that would in principle apply to Tchebycheff contour structure. The Tchebycheff–tropical identification is informal cross-domain analogy at present, not a cited theorem [†caveat-T]; six search queries returned no peer-reviewed paper developing it as a stand-alone result. The right tools, the right language, no theorem yet.

    11.5 Critical Reading#

    Three methodological correctives that help a reader build taste.

    • Akhter, Fahrmann, Sonntag, Peitz, Schwietert (2024), Common Pitfalls to Avoid While Using Multiobjective Optimization in Machine Learning, arXiv:2405.01480. — Catalogues the recurring failure modes that §9 is built around: linear scalarisation on non-convex fronts, Pareto-optimality claims without convergence verification, method comparisons under unequal compute budgets. The most actionable corrective in the literature; the existence of this paper is itself diagnostic of the field's maturity debt.
    • Adler (2019), Measuring Social Welfare: An Introduction, Oxford University Press. — The welfare-economics methodology side of Thread 1. Distinguishes utilitarian, prioritarian, sufficientarian, and Rawlsian aggregators with axiomatic precision; reading it sharpens the recognition that §2.4's scalarisation zoo is a welfare-aggregator zoo with renamed coordinates. The book a deep-learning reader should consult before claiming a Nash-MTL or max-min update is "fair."
    • Casper, Davies, Shi, Gilbert, Scheurer, Rando, Freedman, Korbak, Lindner, Freire, Wang, Marks, Segerie, Carroll, Peng, Christoffersen, Damani, Slocum, Anwar, Siththaranjan, Nadeau, Michaud, Pfau, Krasheninnikov, Chen, Langosco, Hase, Bıyık, Dragan, Krueger, Sadigh, Hadfield-Menell (2023), Open Problems and Fundamental Limitations of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, TMLR (Survey Certification + Featured Certification + Outstanding Paper Finalist). arXiv:2307.15217. — The diagnosis §7 inherits. Names the scalar-reward bottleneck, the annotator-averaging problem, and the auditing gaps that §7.7–§7.13 develop case-by-case. The right reading after §7 for a reader who wants to see the open-problem landscape before §10 narrows it.

A reader entering MOO from one community should treat §11.1 as the on-ramp, §11.2 as the priors they will see rediscovered, §11.3 as the current edge, §11.4 as the lateral threads with their honesty markers, and §11.5 as the discipline that keeps the rest legible.
