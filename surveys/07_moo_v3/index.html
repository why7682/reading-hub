    Multi-Objective Optimization Across Five Communities (v3, LLM-Era 2024–2026)#

    A survey of OR/MCDM, evolutionary MOO, gradient multi-task learning, multi-objective reinforcement learning, and RLHF / LLM alignment, organised around a single thesis: one theorem appears in four communities under four names.

    §1 Introduction and v3 positioning#

    The first two versions of this survey (v1 in 2022, v2 in 2024) read the multi-objective optimisation (MOO) literature as a symmetric five-community map: operations research / multi-criteria decision making (OR/MCDM), evolutionary MOO, gradient multi-task learning (MTL), multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL), and the then-young RLHF-for-LLM strand. v3 keeps that spine but deliberately shifts its centre of gravity.

    By late 2025 the LLM-alignment strand is no longer a peripheral application: it has acquired its own closed-form policy theory (DPO and the \(\Psi\)-PO family), its own constrained-MOO formulation (Safe RLHF and one-shot Lagrangian dualisation), its own parameter-space MOO programme (Rewarded Soups, Panacea), and its own pluralistic-alignment / agentic-MOO and bilevel-PEFT sub-literatures.

    The differential mass between v2 and v3 lives almost entirely in this LLM-era layer (2023 Q4 – 2026 Q1), and v3 is structured to keep that differential mass legible rather than dissolved in a balanced cross-community survey.

    This positioning has a sharper, more interesting consequence. Of the five unifying threads identified in Phase 0b (U1 linear-scalarisation completeness; U2 KKT for vector-valued objectives; U3 gradient-conflict projection geometry; U4 KL-regularised soft-max + Lagrangian duality; U5 preference-conditioned parameter-space MOO via linear mode connectivity), only U5 is genuinely new to the LLM era.

    Specifically: U1 is Geoffrion 1968. U2 is Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (1939 / 1951) lifted to vector-valued objectives, with proper-Pareto refinements due to Geoffrion. U3 is the min-norm projection geometry of MGDA (Désidéri 2012) and its NeurIPS-2018–2023 descendants. U4 is the KL-regularised soft-max + Lagrangian / Donsker–Varadhan computation that goes back at least to soft Q-learning (2017).

    The LLM era has restated U1–U4 inside policy space — the policy \(\pi_\theta\) replaces the decision variable \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\) — but it has not produced new theorems for them.

    U5, by contrast, depends on the empirical observation that SFT-rooted fine-tunes lie inside a single low-loss basin (linear mode connectivity, LMC) and is therefore a genuinely 2024–2026 contribution with no pre-LLM analogue.

    The survey's signature finding — and the load-bearing thesis of v3 — is the four-restatement identity. The same theorem appears in four communities under four names:

    1. OR/MCDM (Geoffrion 1968). Linear scalarisation \(g_{\mathrm{LS}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\) is complete only under convexity. For non-convex feasible regions, linear scalarisation recovers only the convex hull of the Pareto set and misses the non-convex portion of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) entirely. This is the foundational scalarisation-incompleteness theorem.
    2. Gradient MTL (Liu et al. NeurIPS 2021). Among the dominant gradient-MOO methods, only CAGrad guarantees convergence to a stationary point of the average loss \(\bar L=k^{-1}\sum_i L_i\) under non-convex per-task losses. MGDA, MGDA-UB, PCGrad, and Nash-MTL all admit Pareto-stationary points that are not Pareto-optimal when the \(L_i\) are non-convex. The gap between Pareto-stationarity and Pareto-optimality is the gradient-MTL face of (1).
    3. MORL (Roijers et al. JAIR 2013 / Hayes et al. JAAMAS 2022). The convex coverage set \(\mathrm{CCS}\) is the set of policies optimal under some linear utility \(u(\mathbf{V})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{V}\) on the vector value function; the Pareto coverage set \(\mathrm{PCS}\) is the set of policies optimal under some monotone utility. Whenever the user utility is non-linear, \(\mathrm{CCS}\subsetneq\mathrm{PCS}\) strictly, and a linear-utility-trained agent is not in \(\mathrm{PCS}\).
    4. RLHF (Zhou et al. ACL Findings 2024, MODPO). Multi-reward DPO with a linear scalarisation \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{r}\) inside the log-ratio inherits the convex-hull restriction; sweeping \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) on the simplex recovers only the convex hull of the alignment Pareto front, not its non-convex regions where MaxMin-RLHF (Chakraborty et al. ICML 2024) and Tchebycheff-style alignment objectives live.

    Each of (1)–(4) is the same theorem with different decision variables and different ambient geometries: in (1) \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\), in (2) \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d\), in (3) \(\pi\) on a finite MDP, in (4) \(\pi_\theta\) on prompt–response sequences.

    v3 puts this identity at the centre because it is the cleanest cross-community payoff currently available — and because, in the form we state, no 2022–2025 published survey on MOO, MTL, MORL, or RLHF states the identity as a single chained claim. It is the survey's load-bearing claim.

    The remaining structural commitments of v3 follow from the above. Thread A (classical Pareto + evolutionary MOO) is compressed to ≈3,500 words because it is now well-surveyed elsewhere and v3 does not contribute new results in it.

    Thread B (gradient MTL + MORL) is similarly compressed to ≈3,500 words and concentrates its content on the Pareto-stationarity / Pareto-optimality gap that anchors restatement (2) above. Thread C (LLM-era multi-objective alignment) carries the bulk of v3's word budget — ≈7,500 words across §8–§12 — because it is where the four-restatement identity is sharpest and where the 2024–2026 literature is densest.

    §2 Pareto theory and the scalarisation lattice#

    Model basis. Decision space \(\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\) feasible under inequality constraints \(g_j(\mathbf{x})\le 0\) (\(j=1,\ldots,p\)) and equality constraints \(h_l(\mathbf{x})=0\) (\(l=1,\ldots,q\)); objective \(\mathbf{f}:\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\to\mathbb{R}^k\) assumed continuously differentiable unless stated; ordering cone \(C=\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\).

    More precisely: ideal point \(\mathbf{z}^\star\in\mathbb{R}^k\) defined by \(z_i^\star=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}}f_i(\mathbf{x})\) (componentwise infimum, not attained jointly in general); reference point \(\bar{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^k\) user-supplied. Throughout this section we work in decision space; later sections will lift to parameter space (\(\theta\)) and policy space (\(\pi\)).

    §2.1 Cone dominance and three refinement levels#

    Definition 2.1 (Pareto dominance). Given \(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\), \(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\preceq\mathbf{x}^{(2)}\) iff \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\le\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{(2)})\) componentwise and \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\ne\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{(2)})\). Equivalently, \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{(2)})-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\in C\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\) where \(C=\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\).

    A point \(\mathbf{x}^\star\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) is Pareto-optimal iff there is no \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) with \(\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{x}^\star\). The set of all such points is the Pareto-optimal set \(\mathcal{X}^\star\); its image \(\mathcal{P}^\star=\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{X}^\star)\subset\mathbb{R}^k\) is the Pareto front. Plainly: \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is Pareto-optimal iff one cannot improve some \(f_i\) without worsening some other \(f_j\).

    Definition 2.2 (Weak Pareto). \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is weakly Pareto-optimal iff there is no \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) with \(f_i(\mathbf{x})<f_i(\mathbf{x}^\star)\) in every coordinate \(i\). Equivalently, the relation \(\preceq_{\text{w}}\) requires strict inequality coordinatewise.

    Definition 2.3 (Geoffrion-proper Pareto, Geoffrion 1968). \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is Geoffrion-proper Pareto-optimal iff it is Pareto-optimal and there exists a constant \(M>0\) such that for every \(i\) and every \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) with \(f_i(\mathbf{x})<f_i(\mathbf{x}^\star)\), there exists \(j\) with \(f_j(\mathbf{x})>f_j(\mathbf{x}^\star)\) and the marginal rate of substitution $\(\frac{f_i(\mathbf{x}^\star)-f_i(\mathbf{x})}{f_j(\mathbf{x})-f_j(\mathbf{x}^\star)}\;\le\;M.\)$ The condition rules out unbounded trade-offs (a tiny improvement in \(f_i\) purchased at an arbitrarily large worsening of some \(f_j\)) and is the refinement that makes linear-scalarisation completeness work in the convex case.

    The three levels nest as \(\preceq_{\text{w}}\;\supset\;\preceq\;\supset\;\preceq_{\text{prop}}\) — the set of weakly Pareto-optimal points is the largest, properly Pareto-optimal is the smallest. Most scalarisation theorems quoted below are stated for one specific level; v3 will be explicit about which.

    §2.2 Scalarisation completeness theorems P1–P5#

    The scalarisation programme picks a parameterised family of scalar functions \(\{g(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\lambda})\}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda}\) on \(\mathbb{R}^k\) and asks: for which \(\mathbf{x}^\star\in\mathcal{X}^\star\) does there exist \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda\) with \(\mathbf{x}^\star\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}}g(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x});\boldsymbol{\lambda})\)?

    The five canonical answers in classical MOO are theorems P1–P5 below (the P-prefix marks Pareto-scalarisation theorems of §2; cf. K-prefix in §3 for KKT, H-prefix in §4 for hypervolume, M-prefix in §5 for MTL, V-prefix in §7 for vector-value, D-prefix in §8–§9 for DPO, S-prefix in §10 for Safe/Lagrangian, L-prefix in §11 for LMC, X-prefix in §12 for MaxMin / closure). We state hypotheses and conclusions, give one-line proof sketches, and defer their application to the LLM era to §8–§12.

    Theorem P1 (Linear scalarisation, sufficient direction; formerly T1 in §2). Let \(g_{\mathrm{LS}}(\mathbf{f};\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{f}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\), \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}>\mathbf{0}\). If \(\mathbf{x}^\star\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathrm{LS}}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x});\boldsymbol{\lambda})\) exists then \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is Pareto-optimal.

    Proof sketch. If \(\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{x}^\star\) then \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\le\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^\star)\) strictly (since \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}>\mathbf{0}\) and at least one coordinate strictly improves), contradicting optimality. \(\square\)

    Theorem P2 (Linear scalarisation, necessary direction under convexity — Geoffrion 1968; formerly T2 in §2). If \(\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) is convex and each \(f_i\) is convex on \(\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\), then every Geoffrion-proper Pareto-optimal \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is a minimiser of some \(g_{\mathrm{LS}}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\lambda})\) with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\), \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}>\mathbf{0}\).

    Conversely, without the convexity hypothesis the conclusion fails: there exist non-convex Pareto fronts on which entire connected components of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) are inaccessible to any \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\).

    Proof sketch. Convex separation between \(\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}})+C\) and a small open ball around \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^\star)\) exists by the Hahn–Banach theorem; its normal lies in \(\mathbb{R}^k_{\ge 0}\) and after normalisation lies on \(\Delta^{k-1}\). Geoffrion-proper-ness is precisely the bounded-marginal-rate condition that prevents the separating hyperplane from being a degenerate face. \(\square\)

    P2 is the single most-quoted theorem in this survey because it is restatement (1) of the four-restatement identity. The convexity hypothesis matters: when \(\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) or any \(f_i\) is non-convex, the set of \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) recoverable by linear scalarisation is the convex hull of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) — denoted \(\mathrm{CCS}\) when we lift to MORL and to RLHF below — and the non-convex remainder of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) is inaccessible.

    We will see this defect surface again in §6 (only CAGrad recovers \(\bar L\) stationarity), §7 (\(\mathrm{CCS}\subsetneq\mathrm{PCS}\)), §9 (linear MODPO inherits the restriction), and §10 (linear MOCAN inherits the restriction).

    Theorem P3 (Weighted Tchebycheff completeness — Steuer–Choo 1983 / Steuer 1986 / Miettinen 1999; formerly T3 in §2). Let \(g_{\mathrm{TC}}(\mathbf{f};\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{z}^\star)=\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}\lambda_i(f_i-z_i^\star)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\), \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}>\mathbf{0}\), \(\mathbf{z}^\star\) the utopia (ideal) point with \(z_i^\star\le\inf_\mathcal{X} f_i\) so \(f_i\ge z_i^\star\) throughout (the absolute value is then redundant; we follow the Steuer–Choo convention).

    For every Pareto-optimal \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) — convex or non-convex objective — there exists \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) such that \(\mathbf{x}^\star\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}g_{\mathrm{TC}}(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x});\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{z}^\star)\). The conclusion is weak Pareto in general and Pareto under an augmentation \(g_{\mathrm{TC}}^{(\epsilon)}=g_{\mathrm{TC}}+\epsilon\sum_i\lambda_i f_i\) for small \(\epsilon>0\).

    Proof sketch. Each level set \(\{\mathbf{f}:g_{\mathrm{TC}}\le c\}\) is an axis-aligned rectangle anchored at \(\mathbf{z}^\star\); sweeping \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) tilts the rectangle and every Pareto point is on the boundary of some such rectangle. The augmentation \(\epsilon\sum_i\lambda_i f_i\) resolves the weak-Pareto edge case where multiple Pareto points share a Tchebycheff value. \(\square\)

    Theorem P4 (Achievement-scalarising-function (ASF) completeness — Wierzbicki 1980; formerly T4 in §2). Let \(s_{\mathrm{ASF}}(\mathbf{f};\bar{\mathbf{z}},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\rho)=\max_i\lambda_i(f_i-\bar z_i)+\rho\sum_i\lambda_i(f_i-\bar z_i)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}>\mathbf{0}\), \(\rho>0\). For every Pareto-optimal \(\mathbf{x}^\star\), \(\mathbf{x}^\star\in\arg\min s_{\mathrm{ASF}}\) for some choice of reference \(\bar{\mathbf{z}}\).

    The user controls where on the front to land by choosing \(\bar{\mathbf{z}}\); the \(\rho\) term ensures Pareto (not just weak Pareto). ASF generalises P3 — Tchebycheff is the \(\rho\to 0\), \(\bar{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{z}^\star\) specialisation.

    Theorem P5 (\(\varepsilon\)-constraint completeness — Haimes et al. 1971; formerly T5 in §2). Fix index \(i_0\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and bounds \(\varepsilon_j\in\mathbb{R}\) for \(j\ne i_0\); solve \(\min f_{i_0}\) s.t. \(f_j\le\varepsilon_j\) for \(j\ne i_0\). For every Pareto-optimal \(\mathbf{x}^\star\), there exist \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) and \(i_0\) such that \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is the unique minimiser.

    The \(\varepsilon\)-constraint is the non-scalarisation alternative — it converts \(k-1\) objectives into constraints — and it is complete in general (no convexity required).

    The lattice that emerges: P1+P2 are complete only under convexity; P3, P4, P5 are complete in general. P1 is computationally cheapest (single scalar minimisation, no \(\max\)); P3–P5 are computationally heavier (non-smooth \(\max\) or constraint reformulation) but recover the non-convex portion of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\).

    The trade-off — speed vs. completeness — is the structural fact that drives community-by-community algorithm design and that resurfaces in §9–§12 as the choice between linear MODPO and MaxMin-RLHF.

    A note on the relationship between \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) as a user input (the formalism above) and \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) as an algorithm output (the formalism that will dominate §5–§6): in P1–P4 the user picks \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) and the algorithm returns a single Pareto point.

    In gradient MTL, by contrast, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is computed by the algorithm from gradient geometry (MGDA min-norm, PCGrad pairwise projection, etc.) and the user supplies no preference at all; the resulting iterate is one Pareto-stationary point per run.

    The symbol \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is reserved for the algorithm-side throughout, and \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) for the user-side. The distinction is preserved in §11 (Rewarded Soups / Panacea use \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) as architectural input) and §12 (MaxMin-RLHF's Sion-dual reformulation makes \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) an inner-min variable, blurring the line).

    §3 KKT for vector-valued objectives#

    Model basis. Same feasible region \(\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n\) as §2; constraints \(g_j(\mathbf{x})\le 0\), \(h_l(\mathbf{x})=0\); objective \(\mathbf{f}:\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\to\mathbb{R}^k\), all \(f_i,g_j,h_l\) continuously differentiable.

    More precisely, the Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification (MFCQ) at \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) requires: \(\{\nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^\star)\}_{j\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}^\star)}\) positively linearly independent together with \(\{\nabla h_l(\mathbf{x}^\star)\}\) linearly independent (where \(\mathcal{A}\) is the active inequality set).

    §3.1 The four-condition KKT system#

    The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (1939 / 1951) system for scalar-objective constrained optimisation has four conditions: (i) stationarity, (ii) complementary slackness, (iii) primal feasibility, (iv) dual feasibility. For vector-valued objectives the system is the same except that the gradient of the objective is replaced by a convex combination \(\sum_i\alpha_i\nabla f_i\) with \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\).

    Theorem K1 (Necessary KKT for vector-valued objectives — Fritz John, Kuhn–Tucker; refined for proper Pareto by Geoffrion 1968; formerly T6 in §3). Let \(\mathbf{x}^\star\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) be properly Pareto-optimal and let MFCQ hold at \(\mathbf{x}^\star\). Then there exist multipliers \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) (with \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}>\mathbf{0}\) for proper Pareto), \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^p_{\ge 0}\), \(\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^q\) such that:

    (i) Stationarity: \(\quad\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^k\alpha_i\nabla f_i(\mathbf{x}^\star)\,+\,\sum_{j=1}^p\mu_j\nabla g_j(\mathbf{x}^\star)\,+\,\sum_{l=1}^q\nu_l\nabla h_l(\mathbf{x}^\star)\;=\;\mathbf{0}.\)

    (ii) Complementary slackness: \(\quad\mu_j\,g_j(\mathbf{x}^\star)\;=\;0\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,p.\)

    (iii) Primal feasibility: \(\quad g_j(\mathbf{x}^\star)\le 0\;\forall j;\quad h_l(\mathbf{x}^\star)=0\;\forall l.\)

    (iv) Dual feasibility: \(\quad\mu_j\ge 0\;\forall j;\quad \boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\,(\boldsymbol{\alpha}>\mathbf{0}\text{ for proper Pareto}).\)

    Proof sketch. The simplex constraint \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) comes from the fact that proper Pareto-optimality at \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) implies the system \(\{\nabla f_i(\mathbf{x}^\star)\}\) does not span an entire halfspace direction without a non-negative combination summing to zero (Gordan's theorem on linear inequalities); the constraint multipliers come from MFCQ exactly as in the scalar KKT.

    K1 is restatement of U2 in Phase 0b's unifying-thread vocabulary. The simplex multiplier \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is the same object as the MGDA min-norm element of §5, the same object as the Nash-MTL bargaining weight, and (up to sign) the same object as the Lagrange multiplier on the second objective in the constrained-RLHF reformulation of §10.

    More precisely, the four-restatement identity does not start at §1's Geoffrion 1968 — it starts here, in the structure of the simplex multiplier on the objective side of the KKT system.

    Theorem K2 (Sufficient KKT under convexity; formerly T7 in §3). If each \(f_i\) is convex, each \(g_j\) is convex, each \(h_l\) is affine, and \((\mathbf{x}^\star,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\) satisfies (i)–(iv) of K1 with \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) (\(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\ge\mathbf{0}\) for weak Pareto, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}>\mathbf{0}\) for Pareto), then \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is (weakly) Pareto-optimal.

    Proof sketch. For any \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\), the convexity of \(\sum_i\alpha_i f_i\) together with stationarity implies \(\sum_i\alpha_i f_i(\mathbf{x})\ge\sum_i\alpha_i f_i(\mathbf{x}^\star)\), which precludes \(\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{x}^\star\). \(\square\)

    §3.2 The simplex multiplier and what it means downstream#

    The single most important deferred-reading note for §5–§12 is that the simplex multiplier \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) in K1 has at least five different operational realisations across the five communities, each computing the same object by a different subroutine.

    Specifically: in OR/MCDM, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is solved for jointly with \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) via the KKT system itself (typically by an interior-point method on a linearised problem). In evolutionary MOO, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) does not appear explicitly — the population sweeps the front and the simplex multiplier is implicit in the decomposition vectors of MOEA/D.

    In gradient MTL, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is computed by an inner subproblem at each iteration: MGDA solves \(\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}}\|\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_2\) for the min-norm element (§5); PCGrad picks \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) pairwise by conflict cosine; CAGrad picks \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) from a trust-region max–min subroutine; Nash-MTL picks \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) from Nash bargaining axioms.

    In MORL, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is user-supplied as the linear-utility weight \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) (and the user/algorithm distinction collapses for linear utilities). In RLHF, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is again \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) in MODPO (user-supplied) and is the Lagrange dual \(\eta\) in Safe RLHF / one-shot dualisation (algorithm-computed at the constraint boundary).

    This shared substrate is the A↔B stitch of the survey: KKT is the parent, MGDA / PCGrad / CAGrad / Nash-MTL are constructive instantiations on \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d\) via different choices of subroutine for \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\). They differ not in the structure of the KKT system but in the constraint set on the simplex and in the meaning of the resulting iterate (Pareto-stationary vs. stationary of \(\bar L\) — the FC-5 distinction that §6 will explicate).

    The substrate also unifies \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\): in the constrained-RLHF reformulation of §10, where the second objective is rewritten as a constraint \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[C_\psi]\le b\), complementary slackness in K1 reads \(\eta(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[C_\psi]-b)=0\) and the stationarity condition gives \(\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}[R_\phi-\eta C_\psi]+(\text{KL terms})=\mathbf{0}\).

    The Lagrange multiplier \(\eta\) at the constraint boundary plays the role of an optimal scalarisation weight — the second slot of \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) in K1 with the simplex constraint relaxed and the constraint budget \(b\) taking up the slack. The duality between \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\)-on-the-objective-side and \(\eta\)-on-the-constraint-side will be one of the central structural facts of Thread C.

    §4 Evolutionary MOO and Pareto-compliant indicators#

    Model basis. Population \(\mathcal{A}=\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(N)}\}\subset\mathcal{X}_{\text{feas}}\) of size \(N\) (typically \(50\)–\(300\)); reference point \(\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^k\) assumed dominated by every Pareto point of interest; objective images \(\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{A})\subset\mathbb{R}^k\); performance indicators \(I:2^{\mathbb{R}^k}\to\mathbb{R}\) that score archives by their proximity to and spread along \(\mathcal{P}^\star\).

    The community's central question is how to evolve the population so that \(\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{A})\) converges to an empirical approximation of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) — convergence in the sense of Hausdorff distance or in the sense of indicator value.

    Evolutionary MOO is not a 2024–2026 differentiator and v3 compresses it. The four method families below are stated for completeness; algorithmic detail beyond what is needed for the cross-community taxonomy in §S0 is in Thread A §2 of Phase 0c.

    §4.1 NSGA-II/III, MOEA/D, SMS-EMOA, IBEA#

    NSGA-II (Deb, Agrawal, Pratap, Meyarivan; IEEE TEC 6(2):182–197, 2002). Non-dominated sorting + crowding-distance selection. Each generation: sort the union of parent and offspring populations into Pareto fronts \(F_1,F_2,\ldots\), accept the first whole front that fits the next generation's quota, and use crowding distance (the sum of normalised inter-neighbour distances in objective space) to fill the last partial slot. Computational cost \(O(MN^2)\) per generation where \(M=k\) is the number of objectives.

    (Footnote on author spelling: the IEEE Xplore record uses "Agrawal"; some bibliographic exports list "Agarwal" — same author. Per FC-2 we follow the IEEE spelling here and throughout.)

    NSGA-III (Deb, Jain; IEEE TEC 18(4):577–601, 2014). Replaces crowding distance by reference-direction association — a fixed grid of unit vectors on \(\Delta^{k-1}\) — for many-objective problems (\(k\ge 4\)) where crowding distance collapses. The reference directions are themselves a discretised Tchebycheff family in disguise.

    MOEA/D (Zhang, Li; IEEE TEC 11(6):712–731, 2007). Decomposes the front into \(N\) subproblems indexed by a uniform grid of weights \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}\in\Delta^{k-1}\); each subproblem solves \(\min_{\mathbf{x}}g(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x});\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}^\star)\) for a chosen scalarisation \(g\) (Tchebycheff / weighted-sum / penalty-based intersection); neighbouring subproblems share their best solutions via a local-mating rule.

    The framework operationalises P3 (Tchebycheff completeness) at population level — every Pareto point is in principle the optimum of some subproblem, and the grid density bounds the front-coverage discretisation error.

    SMS-EMOA (Beume, Naujoks, Emmerich; EJOR 181(3):1653–1669, 2007) and IBEA (Zitzler, Künzli; PPSN VIII, LNCS 3242:832–842, 2004). Indicator-based methods that replace dominance + crowding by direct optimisation of a performance indicator. SMS-EMOA targets the hypervolume \(\mathcal{H}\) (H2 below); IBEA targets a parametrised binary indicator family \(I_\epsilon\) or \(I_{\mathrm{HD}}\).

    Per FC-1, IBEA's correct origin is Zitzler–Künzli, PPSN VIII 2004, not the 2008 Knowles–Corne–Deb paper occasionally cited in third-party references; we follow the registry throughout.

    §4.2 The hypervolume indicator and strict Pareto compliance#

    Definition H1 (Hypervolume; formerly T8). Given archive \(\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^k\) and reference \(\mathbf{r}\) dominated by every \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{A})\), $\(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A};\mathbf{r})\;=\;\mu_{\mathrm{Leb}}\!\Bigl(\,\bigcup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{A})}\bigl\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^k:\mathbf{a}\preceq\mathbf{z}\preceq\mathbf{r}\bigr\}\Bigr),\)$ the Lebesgue measure of the dominated region between \(\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{A})\) and \(\mathbf{r}\).

    Theorem H2 (Strict Pareto compliance of \(\mathcal{H}\) — Zitzler, Thiele, Laumanns, Fonseca, Grunert da Fonseca, IEEE TEC 7(2):117–132, 2003; formerly T9 in §4). \(\mathcal{H}\) is the unique indicator among widely-used MOO performance indicators with the property that \(\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{A}'\) (every \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{A})\) is dominated by some \(\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}')\in\mathbf{f}(\mathcal{A}')\)) implies \(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A};\mathbf{r})\le\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}';\mathbf{r})\) for every choice of \(\mathbf{r}\).

    Equivalently, \(\mathcal{H}\) never prefers a dominated archive over a dominating one. The proof rests on the inclusion of dominated regions: adding a non-dominated point strictly increases \(\mathcal{H}\), and replacing a point with one that dominates it weakly increases \(\mathcal{H}\).

    H2 explains why SMS-EMOA targets \(\mathcal{H}\) directly: optimising \(\mathcal{H}\) cannot accidentally reward archives that are Pareto-worse. The cost is computational — \(\mathcal{H}\) is \(\#\)P-hard to compute exactly in general \(k\), and SMS-EMOA uses Klee's algorithm (in \(O(N^{k/2+1})\)) or sampling approximations.

    By contrast, IGD (inverted generational distance) — the second most common indicator — requires knowing \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) in advance (which defeats the purpose if \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) is unknown) and is not strictly Pareto-compliant; cases exist where IGD prefers a dominated archive.

    §4.3 LLM-MOEA bridge (2024)#

    The single LLM-era evolutionary MOO contribution worth noting in v3 is LLM4MOEA (Liu, Lin, Wang, Tong, Yuan, Zhang; arXiv 2310.12541, v3 March 2024), which uses an LLM as a black-box search operator inside the MOEA/D loop: the LLM is prompted to propose offspring conditioned on a sliding window of recent parents and on the subproblem weight \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(i)}\). The empirical claim is that LLM-as-operator outperforms standard SBX crossover on small-budget MOO benchmarks (ZDT, DTLZ at low \(k\)).

    This is a use of LLMs inside MOO rather than the reverse direction that dominates Thread C below (use of MOO inside LLM alignment); v3 mentions it only as a bridge point and does not develop it further.

    §5 Gradient MTL: MGDA family and its descendants#

    Model basis. Parameter \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d\) shared across \(k\) tasks; per-task losses \(L_i:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}\) continuously differentiable; stacked-gradient matrix \(\mathbf{G}(\theta)=[\nabla_\theta L_1(\theta)\,|\,\cdots\,|\,\nabla_\theta L_k(\theta)]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\); simplex \(\Delta^{k-1}\) of algorithmic mixing weights \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\).

    More precisely, the iterate update is \(\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\rho_t\,\mathbf{d}_t\) where \(\mathbf{d}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\) is a common descent direction and \(\rho_t\) a step size satisfying Robbins–Monro \(\sum_t\rho_t=\infty,\sum_t\rho_t^2<\infty\). The community's central question: how to choose \(\mathbf{d}_t\) from the \(k\) per-task gradients \(\nabla_\theta L_i\) when they conflict (negative pairwise cosine)?

    Pareto-stationarity certificate. A point \(\theta^\star\) is Pareto-stationary iff there exists \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) with \(\mathbf{G}(\theta^\star)\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}\). K1 (the KKT system specialised to unconstrained vector-valued objectives) says Pareto-stationarity is necessary for Pareto-optimality; K2 says it is also sufficient under convexity of all \(L_i\).

    Under non-convex \(L_i\), Pareto-stationary points include Pareto-optima but also include local Pareto-saddles that are not Pareto-optimal. The FC-5 distinction below is the formal consequence.

    §5.1 MGDA — Désidéri 2012#

    Definition M1 (Multiple-Gradient Descent Algorithm; formerly T1' in §5). At iterate \(\theta_t\), solve $\(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^\star\;=\;\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}}\;\|\mathbf{G}(\theta_t)\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_2^2,\qquad \mathbf{d}_t^\star\;=\;\mathbf{G}(\theta_t)\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^\star,\)$ and update \(\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\rho_t\,\mathbf{d}_t^\star\). The vector \(\mathbf{d}_t^\star\) is the minimum-norm element of the convex hull \(\mathrm{conv}\{\nabla_\theta L_i(\theta_t)\}_{i=1}^k\); equivalently, it is the projection of \(\mathbf{0}\) onto \(\mathrm{conv}\{\nabla_\theta L_i\}\) in the Euclidean inner product.

    Theorem M2 (Common-descent property — Désidéri 2012; formerly T2' in §5). Either (a) \(\mathbf{d}_t^\star=\mathbf{0}\), in which case \(\theta_t\) is Pareto-stationary; or (b) \(\mathbf{d}_t^\star\ne\mathbf{0}\), in which case \(\langle\mathbf{d}_t^\star,\nabla_\theta L_i(\theta_t)\rangle>0\) for every \(i=1,\ldots,k\). The vector \(-\mathbf{d}_t^\star\) is therefore a strict descent direction for every task simultaneously.

    Proof sketch. The KKT conditions of the min-norm subproblem on \(\Delta^{k-1}\) give \(\langle\mathbf{d}_t^\star,\nabla_\theta L_i\rangle\ge\|\mathbf{d}_t^\star\|_2^2\) for every \(i\) with \(\alpha_i^\star>0\) and \(\langle\mathbf{d}_t^\star,\nabla_\theta L_i\rangle\ge 0\) for every \(i\). Either everything is zero (Pareto-stationary) or every inner product is strictly positive.

    The subproblem \(\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}}\|\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_2\) is a small quadratic programme of size \(k\times k\); it is solved at every step.

    MGDA-UB (Sener, Koltun, NeurIPS 2018, arXiv 1810.04650) introduces an upper-bound surrogate that uses a shared-encoder approximation \(\mathbf{G}\approx\mathbf{Z}^\top\mathbf{F}\) (with \(\mathbf{Z}\) the encoder activations and \(\mathbf{F}\) the per-task head Jacobians) to avoid computing \(k\) full backward passes through the encoder; the resulting subproblem is solved in encoder-activation space and is computationally tractable for modern neural nets.

    Theorem M3 (MGDA convergence under convexity — Désidéri 2012; formerly T3' in §5). If each \(L_i\) is convex and \(L^\infty\)-smooth, the MGDA iterates converge to a Pareto-stationary point that is also Pareto-optimal. Under non-convex \(L_i\), convergence is to a Pareto-stationary point that need not be Pareto-optimal — this is the FC-5 distinction.

    §5.2 PCGrad — Yu et al. NeurIPS 2020#

    Definition M4 (PCGrad; formerly T4' in §5). At iterate \(\theta_t\) with per-task gradients \(\mathbf{g}_i=\nabla_\theta L_i(\theta_t)\), for each ordered pair \((i,j)\) with \(\langle\mathbf{g}_i,\mathbf{g}_j\rangle<0\), replace \(\mathbf{g}_i\leftarrow\mathbf{g}_i-\frac{\langle\mathbf{g}_i,\mathbf{g}_j\rangle}{\|\mathbf{g}_j\|_2^2}\mathbf{g}_j\) (orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{g}_i\) onto the hyperplane normal to \(\mathbf{g}_j\)). After processing all conflict pairs (in a random ordering), update \(\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\rho_t\sum_i\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_i\).

    The projection eliminates the destructive component of each gradient that conflicts with another task while preserving its non-conflicting component.

    PCGrad is operationally cheaper than MGDA (no \(k\times k\) QP) but its theoretical guarantee is weaker. It is not an explicit min-norm projection onto \(\mathrm{conv}\{\mathbf{g}_i\}\); instead it is a pairwise descent rule whose fixed points are Pareto-stationary under convexity but may be cosine-traps under non-convex losses: configurations where all pairwise cosines are \(\ge 0\) but the iterate is not Pareto-stationary in the MGDA sense.

    §5.3 CAGrad — Liu et al. NeurIPS 2021#

    Definition M5 (CAGrad; formerly T5' in §5). Given the average gradient \(\bar{\mathbf{g}}=k^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf{g}_i=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1}/k\), CAGrad solves the trust-region max–min subproblem $\(\mathbf{d}_t^\star\;=\;\arg\max_{\mathbf{d}:\,\|\mathbf{d}-\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|_2\le c\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|_2}\;\min_{i=1,\ldots,k}\;\langle\mathbf{d},\mathbf{g}_i\rangle,\)$ with \(c\in[0,1)\) a trust-region radius. The update \(\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\rho_t\,\mathbf{d}_t^\star\) is the descent step.

    Theorem M6 (CAGrad average-loss stationarity — Liu et al. NeurIPS 2021; formerly T6' in §5). Under non-convex \(L_i\) with bounded second moment \(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla L_i\|_2^2\le G^2\) and gradient Lipschitz constant \(\ell>0\) (bounded smoothness), and with the trust-region radius \(c\in[0,1)\) strictly less than 1, CAGrad's iterates converge to a stationary point of the average loss \(\bar L=k^{-1}\sum_i L_i\) — i.e. \(\nabla_\theta\bar L=k^{-1}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{0}\).

    The two hypotheses are individually load-bearing. \(c<1\) is essential because the trust-region constraint \(\|\mathbf{d}-\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|_2\le c\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|_2\) at \(c=1\) admits \(\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{0}\) as a feasible solution and the descent guarantee on \(\bar L\) collapses. \(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla L_i\|_2^2\le G^2\) is essential in the stochastic regime to control the variance of the per-step descent on \(\bar L\) so that the Robbins–Monro \(\sum\rho_t^2<\infty\) summability suffices for \(\liminf_t\|\nabla\bar L(\theta_t)\|=0\).

    Proof sketch. The trust-region constraint with \(c<1\) ensures \(\mathbf{d}_t^\star\) stays within a controlled neighbourhood of \(\bar{\mathbf{g}}\) with \(\langle\mathbf{d}_t^\star,\bar{\mathbf{g}}\rangle\ge(1-c)\|\bar{\mathbf{g}}\|_2^2>0\); the inner \(\min_i\) ensures every task is non-decreasing along \(-\mathbf{d}_t^\star\); together with the bounded-second-moment hypothesis these give descent on \(\bar L\) with bounded variance, which under smoothness yields convergence to \(\nabla\bar L=\mathbf{0}\).

    This is the central claim formalised by FC-5 in §6 below. CAGrad guarantees stationary of \(\bar L\), a strictly stronger property than Pareto-stationary. MGDA / MGDA-UB / PCGrad / Nash-MTL guarantee only the latter under non-convex \(L_i\).

    §5.4 Nash-MTL — Navon et al. ICML 2022#

    Definition M7 (Nash-MTL; formerly T7' in §5). Treats the gradient choice as a cooperative bargaining problem: each task \(i\) is a player with utility \(u_i(\mathbf{d})=\langle\mathbf{d},\mathbf{g}_i\rangle/\|\mathbf{g}_i\|_2\) (the per-task descent rate), the strategy space is \(\{\mathbf{d}:\|\mathbf{d}\|_2\le 1\}\), and the Nash bargaining solution picks \(\mathbf{d}_t^\star=\arg\max_{\mathbf{d}}\prod_i u_i(\mathbf{d})\) over the strategy space (logarithmic concave product).

    Equivalently, \(\mathbf{d}_t^\star=\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^\star\) with \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^\star\) solving \(\sum_i\alpha_i^{-1}=\sum_i\langle\mathbf{g}_i,\mathbf{d}^\star\rangle/u_i\) — a fixed-point equation.

    Nash-MTL satisfies the four Nash bargaining axioms (Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance, independence of irrelevant alternatives). It is the same \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) object as MGDA's min-norm element, computed via different first-order conditions. Like MGDA, its convergence guarantee is to a Pareto-stationary point under non-convex losses, not to a Pareto-optimum.

    §5.5 Pareto MTL and EPO — preference-aligned variants#

    Pareto MTL (Lin, Zhen, Li, Zhang, Kwong; NeurIPS 2019, proceedings hash 685bfde0...). Decomposes the simplex \(\Delta^{k-1}\) into \(K\) regions defined by reference vectors \(\mathbf{u}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{(K)}\), each pointing into a cone of the objective space; runs \(K\) separate MGDA-like solvers, each constrained to its assigned cone. Output is \(K\) Pareto-stationary points spread across the front rather than a single one. Per FC-16, the canonical citation is the NeurIPS 2019 proceedings page (hash 685bfde0...) rather than an arXiv id, which is left unverified in our registry.

    EPO (Mahapatra, Rajan; ICML 2020). Exact Pareto-Optimal direction-seeking: at each iterate, projects the gradient onto a preference ray \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\), recovering a Pareto-stationary point whose objective vector points along \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). EPO and Pareto MTL together populate the preference-aligned-MTL slot (row 11 in §S0).

    FAMO (Liu, Feng, Stone, Liu; NeurIPS 2023). Fast multi-objective adaptive optimisation; computes loss-balanced weights \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t\) adaptively from loss values rather than from gradient norms or gradient products, removing the per-step \(O(k)\) backward passes that MGDA and Nash-MTL require. Empirically competitive on Cityscapes / NYU-v2 multi-task benchmarks at a fraction of MGDA's wall-clock cost.

    §5.6 Loss-weighting baselines#

    GradNorm (Chen, Badrinarayanan, Lee, Rabinovich; ICML 2018, arXiv 1711.02257). Adjusts task-loss weights \(w_i\) via a learnable gradient-norm balancing objective; the auxiliary loss penalises imbalance in \(\|w_i\nabla L_i\|\) across tasks.

    Uncertainty Weighting (Kendall, Gal, Cipolla; CVPR 2018, arXiv 1705.07115). Treats \(w_i\) as homoscedastic task uncertainties \(\sigma_i^{-2}\), learned jointly with \(\theta\) via a Gaussian-likelihood interpretation \(L_i^{\mathrm{eff}}=L_i/(2\sigma_i^2)+\log\sigma_i\).

    Both are loss-weighting baselines for the MGDA-family methods; neither solves the simplex subproblem for \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) and neither gives the Pareto-stationarity certificate.

    They are empirical balancers with no Pareto guarantee — useful in practice as cheap drop-in alternatives, but not in the same theoretical category as MGDA / PCGrad / CAGrad / Nash-MTL.

    §6 The Pareto-stationarity-vs-Pareto-optimality finding (FC-5)#

    Model basis. Parameter \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^d\) shared across \(k\) tasks; per-task losses \(L_i:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}\) continuously differentiable, \(L\)-smooth, generically non-convex; stacked gradient \(\mathbf{G}(\theta)=[\nabla_\theta L_i]_{i=1}^k\); simplex \(\Delta^{k-1}\) of algorithm weights \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\).

    Pareto-stationarity certificate: \(\exists\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) with \(\mathbf{G}(\theta^\star)\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}\); geometrically, \(\mathbf{0}\in\mathrm{conv}\{\nabla_\theta L_i(\theta^\star)\}\). Under non-convex \(L_i\) this includes local Pareto-saddles reached by gradient cancellation rather than every \(\nabla L_i\) being individually small.

    The single sharpest differential contribution of Thread B — and restatement (2) of the four-restatement identity — is the gap between Pareto-stationarity and Pareto-optimality under non-convex losses. The following hypothesis-distinction table is the authoritative statement of FC-5.

    Method Smoothness req. Convexity req. Iterate guarantee under stated hypotheses
    MGDA (Désidéri 2012) \(L_i\in C^1\), bounded smoothness none for stationarity; convex \(L_i\) for optimality Pareto-stationary; not Pareto-optimal in general non-convex case
    MGDA-UB (Sener–Koltun 2018) as MGDA + shared-encoder approximation as MGDA Pareto-stationary of an upper bound; same FC-5 caveat
    PCGrad (Yu et al. 2020) \(L_i\in C^1\) none for fixed-points; convex for optimality Fixed points include Pareto-stationary points + cosine-traps
    CAGrad (Liu et al. 2021) \(L_i\in C^1\), bounded smoothness none required Stationary of \(\bar L=k^{-1}\sum_i L_i\) — strictly stronger than Pareto-stationary
    Nash-MTL (Navon et al. 2022) \(L_i\in C^1\) convex \(L_i\) for Nash-equilibrium → Pareto-optimality Pareto-stationary in the Nash sense; same FC-5 caveat under non-convexity

    The asymmetry is structural. Pareto-stationarity asks only that the zero vector lie in \(\mathrm{conv}\{\nabla_\theta L_i\}\) — a first-order condition that is necessary for Pareto-optimality but in general not sufficient.

    Stationarity of \(\bar L\), by contrast, asks that \(k^{-1}\sum_i\nabla_\theta L_i=\mathbf{0}\), which under bounded smoothness and the trust-region constraint of CAGrad is reached at iterates where the single combination \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=k^{-1}\mathbf{1}\) certifies stationarity. The CAGrad iterate at convergence is therefore Pareto-stationary with the uniform simplex weight and is locally a stationary point of \(\bar L\) — a strictly stronger guarantee.

    This is restatement (2) of the four-restatement identity because the gap between "MGDA Pareto-stationarity" (rows 1, 7 of §S0) and "CAGrad \(\bar L\) stationarity" (row 9 of §S0) is a structural analogue of the gap between "linear scalarisation reaches only the convex hull" (Geoffrion 1968, P2) and "Tchebycheff scalarisation reaches the full Pareto front" (Steuer 1986, P3) in §2.

    In both cases, the linear / uniform-simplex combination of the objectives is incomplete under non-convexity; the non-linear / max-style combination escapes the incompleteness.

    Two distinct gaps; do not conflate. The FC-5 gap (Pareto-stationary vs Pareto-optimal) is a first-order vs zeroth-order certificate gap in \(\theta\)-space — local Pareto-saddles satisfy the first-order certificate but are not Pareto-optima. The Geoffrion 1968 gap (convex hull vs full Pareto front) is a image-space convex-hull restriction under non-convex \(\mathbf{L}(\theta)\).

    More precisely: CAGrad's iterate uses fixed \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=k^{-1}\mathbf{1}\) and is itself a linear scalarisation of \(\bar L\); it escapes the FC-5 gap (its iterate is \(\bar L\)-stationary, not merely Pareto-stationary) but inherits Geoffrion's convex-hull restriction — its reachable iterate sits inside \(\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{P}^\star)\). Restatement (2) is thus a structural analogy via the shared KKT simplex-multiplier substrate (§3.2), not a strict logical reduction of (1).

    We will see restatement (3) (\(\mathrm{CCS}\subsetneq\mathrm{PCS}\)) in §7 and restatement (4) (MODPO convex-hull defect) in §9, completing the identity.

    §7 MORL: vector value function, CCS vs PCS, envelope methods, continual MORL#

    Model basis. Multi-objective Markov decision process \((\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,\boldsymbol{r},\gamma)\) where \(\boldsymbol{r}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^k\) is a vector-valued reward and the discount \(\gamma\in[0,1)\). A policy \(\pi:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})\) induces the vector value function $\(\mathbf{V}^\pi(s)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_\pi\!\Bigl[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\boldsymbol{r}(s_t,a_t)\,\Big|\,s_0=s\Bigr]\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^k,\)$ and a utility function \(u:\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}\) collapses \(\mathbf{V}^\pi\) to a scalar. The user supplies \(u\); the algorithm picks \(\pi\) to maximise \(u(\mathbf{V}^\pi)\). Pareto-dominance on \(\mathbf{V}^\pi\) is the componentwise relation as in §2; Pareto-optimal policies are those whose value vectors are non-dominated.

    The community's central question — and the source of restatement (3) — is: under which utility functions does linear scalarisation recover the whole space of policies the user might want?

    §7.1 The CCS vs PCS distinction (Roijers JAIR 2013 / Hayes JAAMAS 2022)#

    Definition V1 (Convex coverage set and Pareto coverage set; formerly T9' in §7). The convex coverage set \(\mathrm{CCS}\) is the set of value vectors \(\mathbf{V}^\pi\) such that \(\pi\) is optimal for some linear utility \(u_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{V})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{V}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\). The Pareto coverage set \(\mathrm{PCS}\) is the set of value vectors \(\mathbf{V}^\pi\) such that \(\pi\) is optimal for some monotone increasing utility \(u:\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}\) (non-linear permitted).

    Theorem V2 (\(\mathrm{CCS}\subseteq\mathrm{PCS}\), strict under non-linear utility; formerly T10 in §7). \(\mathrm{CCS}\subseteq\mathrm{PCS}\) always. The containment is strict whenever the Pareto front of \(\{\mathbf{V}^\pi\}\) has non-convex regions and the user utility is non-linear monotone (e.g. Tchebycheff \(u(\mathbf{V})=\min_i\lambda_i V_i\), or any concave monotone \(u\) with curvature).

    Restatement: an MORL agent trained by sweeping linear utilities \(u_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\) visits only \(\mathrm{CCS}\); if the deployed user's utility is non-linear monotone, the trained agent may be sub-optimal in \(\mathrm{PCS}\setminus\mathrm{CCS}\).

    V2 is restatement (3) of the four-restatement identity. The proof reduces to P2 above: linear scalarisation on \(\mathbf{V}^\pi\) inherits Geoffrion 1968's convex-hull restriction; the convex hull of the value-vector Pareto front is exactly \(\mathrm{CCS}\); the non-convex remainder is exactly \(\mathrm{PCS}\setminus\mathrm{CCS}\).

    The MORL community's name for the same theorem is different — they call \(\mathrm{CCS}\subsetneq\mathrm{PCS}\) the non-linear utility gap — but the underlying mathematics is identical.

    The Hayes et al. JAAMAS 2022 practical-guide paper (eighteen-author roster) recasts Roijers et al. JAIR 2013's taxonomy in utility-profile language: rather than distinguishing single-policy vs. multi-policy MORL, distinguish known linear utility (a single \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) at training time → CCS member sufficient), known non-linear utility (a fixed monotone \(u\) → PCS member sufficient), and unknown utility at training time (must learn the full PCS or a coverage approximation thereof).

    The utility-profile axis is orthogonal to the policy-cardinality axis and a more useful taxonomy for the LLM-alignment lift in §11–§12.

    §7.2 Envelope Q-learning — Yang, Sun, Narasimhan NeurIPS 2019#

    Envelope Q-learning (Yang, Sun, Narasimhan; NeurIPS 2019, arXiv 1908.08342). A single neural network represents the vector-valued Q function \(\mathbf{Q}^\pi(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^k\), conditioned on the user-side preference \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) as an input. Training uses a generalised envelope Bellman update $\(\mathbf{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(s,a)\;\leftarrow\;\boldsymbol{r}(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbf{V}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(s'),\qquad \mathbf{V}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(s')=\max_{a',\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}'}\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}'}(s',a'),\)$ where the inner \(\max\) over auxiliary \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}'\) sweeps the simplex of plausible deployment preferences. The network at convergence parameterises the entire CCS by \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\); switching preferences at deployment is a forward pass, not a retrain.

    Envelope Q-learning's front coverage is CCS, not PCS: the linear utility \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{Q}\) at the heart of the envelope update is exactly the linear scalarisation that Geoffrion 1968 / V2 says recovers only the convex hull. To reach the non-convex portion of the Pareto front, the envelope update must be replaced by a non-linear-utility analogue — which is open as of v3's cut-off.

    §7.3 Continual MORL — CORe3 (Huang et al. IJCAI 2024 #490)#

    CORe3 (Huang et al. IJCAI 2024 paper #490). Continual MORL with a reward-model rehearsal buffer: when a new objective \(r_{k+1}\) is added at task time, the agent replays trajectories under the previous reward models \(\boldsymbol{r}_{1:k}\) and uses a regularised CCS-tracking objective to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

    The construction is operational rather than theoretical; the formal guarantees on CCS preservation under continual addition are stated only empirically. Per FC-14, the full author chain is not verified in our registry and we cite by anchor "CORe3 (Huang et al. IJCAI 2024 paper #490)" only.

    §7.4 A note on the Boldi et al. position paper#

    Boldi et al. arXiv 2410.11221 (Oct 2024). Position paper arguing for MORL as the formally correct framework for pluralistic alignment. The paper sits between MORL (Thread B) and pluralistic alignment (Thread C, §12 below) — its argument is that the value-vector \(\mathbf{V}^\pi\) formalism is strictly more expressive than reward-mixture-model approaches to pluralism. Per FC-15, the full author list is not verified in our registry; we cite by anchor "Boldi et al. arXiv 2410.11221".

    §7.5 The B↔C stitch: from MORL to LLM alignment#

    The transition from §7 to §8 is conceptually simple but technically loaded. The MORL formalism above operates on a finite-action MDP with vector reward \(\boldsymbol{r}(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^k\); the LLM-alignment formalism of §8–§12 operates on a (typically token-level) MDP with prompt \(s\) and response \(a\), with \(a\) either a single token or a full sequence depending on the granularity.

    The vector reward survives the lift — multi-reward DPO, MaxMin-RLHF, and Safe RLHF all use \(\mathbf{r}(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^k\) or split reward / cost — but two new features appear that have no clean MORL analogue: (i) a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (the SFT model) that anchors the policy via KL regularisation, and (ii) the closed-form relationship between the KL-regularised reward-maximising policy and its log-ratio (Theorem D1 of §8 below), which makes the entire DPO family tractable.

    The MORL V2 statement of \(\mathrm{CCS}\subsetneq\mathrm{PCS}\) lifts directly to RLHF as restatement (4) below — MODPO's linear scalarisation inherits the convex-hull restriction — and is the same theorem by the same proof.

    The deeper connection between Thread B and Thread C lives in the gradient-MTL / MODPO bridge noted in Phase 0c. Nash-MTL's bargaining solution (§5.4) and a hypothetical multi-reward DPO with algorithmic-side \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) (rather than user-side \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\)) are mathematically the same construct, applied to different gradients: Nash bargaining on \(u_i(\mathbf{d})=\langle\mathbf{d},\nabla_\theta L_i\rangle\) in MTL versus Nash bargaining on \(u_i(\mathbf{d})=\langle\mathbf{d},\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[r_i]\rangle\) in algorithmic-\(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) MODPO.

    Phase 0d's stitch lemma flags this as a hypothesised equivalence pending audit of a specific Nash-bargaining-MODPO paper; v3 carries the hypothesis as a structural claim and does not assert the full formal equivalence (loss-scale invariance, IIA, symmetry, Pareto efficiency all transfer) without that audit.

    §8 Closed-form DPO family#

    Model basis. Policy \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) parameterised by \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^p\), where \(s\) is a prompt context (often \(x\) in alignment papers) and \(a\) is a response token or sequence (\(y\)). Reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the supervised-fine-tuned (SFT) model that fixes the anchor of the KL regulariser.

    More precisely: KL temperature \(\beta>0\); scalar reward model \(r_\phi(s,a)\) learned from a preference dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{(s,a^+,a^-)\}\) under a Bradley–Terry pairwise model. The KL term is the reverse form $\(\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid s)]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\!\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)}\Bigr];\)$ direction is the form used throughout §8–§12 and is not swapped with the forward \(\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\text{ref}}\,\|\,\pi_\theta]\) anywhere. The single-reward KL-regularised RLHF objective is $\(\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho,\,a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\![r_\phi(s,a)]\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}\![\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid s)]],\qquad \beta>0.\)$

    §8.1 DPO — Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning, Finn NeurIPS 2023#

    Theorem D1 (DPO closed form, U4 specialisation; formerly T1 in §8). For the per-state optimisation \(\max_\pi\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[r(s,a)]\,-\,\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid s)]\) with \(\pi,\pi_{\text{ref}}\) distributions on the response space \(\mathcal{A}(s)\), the unique optimum is $\(\pi^\star(a\mid s)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z(s)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)\,\exp\!\bigl(r(s,a)/\beta\bigr),\qquad Z(s)=\sum_{a'\in\mathcal{A}(s)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(a'\mid s)\exp(r(s,a')/\beta).\)$ Proof sketch (Rafailov et al. NeurIPS 2023 Appendix A). Form the per-state Lagrangian \(\mathcal{J}(\pi,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[r]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]-\mu(\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)-1)\) with normalisation multiplier \(\mu\). Variation with respect to \(\pi(a\mid s)\) yields \(r(s,a)-\beta[\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})+1]-\mu=0\), i.e. \(\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})=r/\beta-1-\mu/\beta\). Absorbing the constant \(-1-\mu/\beta\) into \(-\log Z(s)\) via the normalisation \(\sum_a\pi=1\) gives \(\log(\pi^\star/\pi_{\text{ref}})=r/\beta-\log Z(s)\), equivalent to D1.

    The reverse KL direction is essential: forward KL \(\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\text{ref}}\,\|\,\pi_\theta]\) produces a \(-\pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi\) integrand under variation rather than \(\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\), no soft-max closed form follows, and the resulting fixed point would be mode-covering rather than mode-seeking. \(\square\)

    The closed form D1 is the mathematical engine of the whole closed-form DPO family. Every later result in §8 — IPO, KTO, MODPO, Smooth-Tchebycheff-DPO — applies D1 with a different choice of effective reward \(r\) inside the exponential.

    Theorem D2 (DPO log-ratio reparameterisation; formerly T2 in §8). Define the implicit reward $\(r_\theta(s,a)\;:=\;\beta\,\log\!\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)}\;+\;\beta\log Z(s).\)$ For two responses \(a^+,a^-\in\mathcal{A}(s)\), the Bradley–Terry preference probability \(P(a^+\succ a^-\mid s)=\sigma(r(s,a^+)-r(s,a^-))\) has the partition \(Z(s)\) cancel in the difference \(r_\theta(s,a^+)-r_\theta(s,a^-)\). The DPO loss is therefore $\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)\;=\;-\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a^+,a^-)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Bigl[\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a^+\mid s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a^+\mid s)}\,-\,\beta\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a^-\mid s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a^-\mid s)}\Bigr)\Bigr],\)$ and is implementable without ever computing \(Z(s)\) — the entire training loop reduces to a logistic regression on log-likelihood ratios.

    The structural significance of D2 is that DPO removes the reward-model fitting stage that PPO-RLHF (Christiano et al. 2017 / Ouyang et al. 2022) requires: the reward \(r_\theta\) is implicitly defined by the policy ratio \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}}\), and the policy is trained directly from pairwise preference data.

    The implicit reward is bounded on \(\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})\) — but on deterministic preference labels (where \(P(a^+\succ a^-\mid s)\) saturates at 0 or 1) the implicit reward can grow unbounded, and the policy can over-optimise on rare or out-of-distribution responses. This pathology is the motivation for IPO below.

    §8.2 IPO — Gheshlaghi Azar, Z. D. Guo, Piot, Munos, Rowland, Valko, Calandriello AISTATS 2024#

    The \(\Psi\)-PO family. IPO generalises DPO by replacing the log-sigmoid loss with a general link function \(\Psi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\): $\(\mathcal{L}_{\Psi\text{-PO}}(\theta)\;=\;-\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a^+,a^-)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\bigl[\Psi(P_\theta(a^+\succ a^-\mid s))\bigr],\)$ with \(P_\theta\) the Bradley–Terry probability built from \(r_\theta\). DPO is \(\Psi(p)=\log p\); the identity choice \(\Psi(p)=p\) recovers the Identity Preference Optimisation (IPO) loss.

    Theorem D3 (IPO regularised loss; formerly T3 in §8). With identity \(\Psi\) and an inverse-temperature \(\tau>0\), the IPO loss is $\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{IPO}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a^+,a^-)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\Bigl[\Bigl(h_\theta(s,a^+,a^-)\,-\,\tfrac{1}{2\tau}\Bigr)^2\Bigr],\)$ $\(h_\theta(s,a^+,a^-)\;=\;\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a^+\mid s)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(a^-\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a^-\mid s)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(a^+\mid s)}.\)$ The squared loss bounds the implicit reward gap to \(1/(2\tau)\), preventing the over-optimisation pathology of DPO on deterministic preferences: when \(P(a^+\succ a^-\mid s)\to 1\), DPO's log-sigmoid drives \(r_\theta(s,a^+)-r_\theta(s,a^-)\to\infty\), while IPO's squared loss caps it at \(1/(2\tau)\). The trade-off is that IPO trains a bounded-gap implicit reward rather than DPO's unbounded one — better-behaved on saturating preference data but with a smaller effective reward range.

    Venue note (FC-3). IPO is published at AISTATS 2024, verified via proceedings.mlr.press v238 (gheshlaghi-azar24a); the arXiv landing page (2310.12036) does not itself state the AISTATS venue.

    The PMLR v238 byline order — Mohammad Gheshlaghi Azar, Zhaohan Daniel Guo, Bilal Piot, Rémi Munos, Mark Rowland, Michal Valko, Daniele Calandriello — is the canonical one; the family name is rendered "Gheshlaghi Azar" (unhyphenated) on PMLR and arXiv. We follow the PMLR record throughout.

    §8.3 KTO — Ethayarajh, Xu, Muennighoff, Jurafsky, Kiela ICML 2024#

    Kahneman–Tversky Optimisation (KTO). Generalises preference alignment to human-aware losses (HALOs) modelled by Kahneman–Tversky prospect theory: a piecewise utility \(u(\Delta)=\) if \(\Delta\ge 0\) then \((\Delta)^{\alpha_+}\) else \(-\lambda(-\Delta)^{\alpha_-}\), with \(\alpha_+,\alpha_-\in(0,1]\) encoding diminishing sensitivity and \(\lambda>1\) encoding loss aversion.

    KTO requires only binary labels \(\{+,-\}\) per response (not pairwise comparisons), shapes the implicit reward via the piecewise utility, and recovers DPO as a special case when the utility is linear and symmetric.

    KTO's structural significance is its data efficiency: pairwise preference data is expensive to collect; binary "thumbs up / thumbs down" data is plentiful. The closed-form policy still lives inside the D1 family — KTO is a \(\Psi\)-PO instance with a Kahneman–Tversky link — so the entire §8 machinery applies.

    §8.4 RPO and the unified \(\Psi\)-PO framework#

    RPO unified framework (arXiv 2502.00203, Jan 2025). Per FC-13, the author chain is not verified in our registry; we cite by paper anchor. RPO casts DPO, IPO, KTO, and SimPO under a single \(\Psi\)-PO umbrella, parameterised by the link function \(\Psi\) and the regularisation strength. The paper's contribution is the unification proofs (each method is shown to be a specific \(\Psi\) choice) rather than a new algorithm; v3 references RPO as the meta-survey of the closed-form DPO slot.

    §8.5 The slot-1 taxonomy#

    Slot 1 of Thread C — "closed-form DPO family" — is unified by the D1 closed form \(\pi^\star\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)\) and parameterised by (i) the link function \(\Psi\), (ii) the data regime (pairwise / pairwise-with-tie / binary), and (iii) the regularisation choice (log-sigmoid vs. squared vs. Kahneman–Tversky).

    All members share the single-reward assumption. The next section (§9) lifts to multi-reward and exposes the convex-hull restriction (restatement 4 of the four-restatement identity).

    §9 Multi-reward DPO and the MODPO convex-hull restriction#

    Model basis. Vector reward \(\mathbf{r}(s,a)=(r_1(s,a),\ldots,r_k(s,a))\in\mathbb{R}^k\) available either as \(k\) separate reward models \(\{r_{\phi_i}\}\) or as a single multi-headed reward model. User preference \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) is exogenous: the user picks how to weight helpfulness vs. harmlessness vs. brevity vs. truthfulness. KL temperature \(\beta>0\) and reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) as in §8. The multi-reward analogue of the §8 objective is $\(\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{s,\,a\sim\pi_\theta}\!\bigl[g(\mathbf{r}(s,a);\boldsymbol{\lambda})\bigr]\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb{E}_s[\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]],\)$ for some scalarisation \(g:\mathbb{R}^k\times\Delta^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}\). The choice \(g=g_{\mathrm{LS}}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{r}\) gives MODPO; the choice \(g=g_{\mathrm{TC}}=\max_i\lambda_i(r_i-z_i^\star)\) (Steuer–Choo convention, \(\mathbf{z}^\star\) the utopia point of \(\mathbf{r}\); cf. P3) is the open Tchebycheff-DPO target (D5 below).

    §9.1 Linear MODPO — Zhou, Liu, Yang, Shao, Liu, Yue, Ouyang, Qiao Findings of ACL 2024#

    Theorem D4 (Linear-scalarised multi-reward DPO closed form; formerly T4 in §9). For \(\mathbf{r}(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}^k\) and \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\), the linear scalarisation \(g_{\mathrm{LS}}(\mathbf{r};\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{r}\) inside D1 gives $\(\pi^\star(a\mid s)\;\propto\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)\,\exp\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{r}(s,a)/\beta\bigr),\)$ and the MODPO loss replaces the implicit reward \(r_\theta\) of D2 with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{r}_\theta\): $\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{MODPO}}(\theta;\boldsymbol{\lambda})\;=\;-\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a^+,a^-)}\!\Bigl[\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\Bigl(\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a^+\mid s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a^+\mid s)}-\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a^-\mid s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a^-\mid s)}\Bigr)\mathbf{1}\Bigr)\Bigr],\)$ or more directly via per-objective implicit rewards \(r_{\theta,i}=\beta\log(\pi_{\theta,i}/\pi_{\text{ref}})+\beta\log Z_i\).

    The published MODPO paper (Zhou et al. Findings of ACL 2024, arXiv 2310.03708) folds \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) into a single policy network (no per-objective head) and reports better stability than direct MORLHF on the helpfulness / harmlessness Pareto front.

    Limitation — restatement (4) of the four-restatement identity. By Theorem P2 of §2 (Geoffrion 1968), sweeping \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) in D4 recovers only the convex hull of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\) in the alignment value-vector space \(\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\mathbf{r}]\}\).

    More precisely, non-convex regions of the alignment Pareto front — formally, \(\mathcal{P}^\star_{\mathrm{nc}}:=\{\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{P}^\star:\mathbf{f}\notin\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{P}^\star)\}\), where the level-set boundary exhibits inward curvature toward the utopia point — are inaccessible to linear MODPO no matter how finely \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is gridded.

    This is the MODPO completeness defect and is the alignment-community face of the same theorem that produced restatements (1)–(3) above. Phase 0a SP2 names it; Phase 0d §1 enumerates it; the operational evidence on real LLM alignment fronts is in §12 (HELM-Safety pareto-gap discussion) and in seam 1 of §13.

    The venue specifically: MODPO is Findings of ACL 2024 (aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.630), not main ACL. We cite per the ACL Anthology record throughout.

    §9.2 Tchebycheff-DPO and the smooth-Tchebycheff surrogate#

    Theorem D5 (Tchebycheff-DPO open status; formerly T5 in §9). No closed form analogous to D4 is currently known for the weighted Tchebycheff scalarisation \(g_{\mathrm{TC}}(\mathbf{r};\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{z}^\star)=\max_i\lambda_i(r_i-z_i^\star)\) (utopia-anchored Steuer–Choo convention, cf. P3).

    The separability obstacle (relocated from §12; deep-diver P1). The reason for D5's open status is structural and lives properly here in §9.2 rather than in §12's closure.

    D1's closed form depends on additive separability of the reward in the exponential: when the per-state reward decomposes per-token, \(r(s,a_{1:T})=\sum_t r_t(s,a_t)\) and the exponential factorises as \(\exp(r/\beta)=\prod_t\exp(r_t/\beta)\), the policy \(\pi^\star\) factorises into per-token soft-max conditionals and is tractable to sample.

    The \(\max_i\) in \(g_{\mathrm{TC}}\) destroys this additive separability: \(\max_i\lambda_i(r_i-z_i^\star)\) is not a sum of per-token contributions across \(i\), so \(\exp(g_{\mathrm{TC}}/\beta)\) is not a product, and the KKT variation that produces D1 no longer yields a soft-max policy.

    Equivalently, the Bradley–Terry partition \(Z(s)\) that cancels in the D2 pairwise loss depends on additive separability; the \(\max\) form leaves a residual \(Z\) that does not cancel, and a clean log-ratio analogue of D2 is unavailable. Resolving the separability obstacle is the technical heart of seam 1 (Tchebycheff-DPO closed form) of §13.

    The smooth-Tchebycheff surrogate below is the standard attack angle precisely because the LogSumExp is partially separable (it is a \(\log\)-of-sum-of-products, which has a soft-max gradient structure even though it is not strictly additive in the rewards).

    The smooth-Tchebycheff attack angle (Lin, Zhang, Yang, Liu, Wang, Zhang ICML 2024, arXiv 2402.19078). Replace \(g_{\mathrm{TC}}\) by the LogSumExp surrogate $\(g_{\mathrm{ST}}^{(\mu)}(\mathbf{r};\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{z}^\star)\;=\;\mu\,\log\!\sum_{i=1}^k\exp\!\bigl(\lambda_i(r_i-z_i^\star)/\mu\bigr),\)$ which approaches \(g_{\mathrm{TC}}\) as \(\mu\to 0^+\) and is everywhere differentiable for \(\mu>0\). Plugging \(g_{\mathrm{ST}}^{(\mu)}\) into D1 gives the candidate closed form $\(\pi^\star(a\mid s)\;\propto\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)\,\exp\!\bigl(g_{\mathrm{ST}}^{(\mu)}(\mathbf{r}(s,a);\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{z}^\star)/\beta\bigr).\)$

    The remaining theoretical question — flagged as open seam 1 in Phase 0d §8 — is whether the Bradley–Terry-style implicit-reward parameterisation analogous to D2 still holds; that is, whether the partition \(Z(s)\) cancels in the corresponding pairwise loss. The cancellation works for linear and per-token-additive rewards; the smooth-max surrogate is not additive, so a clean log-ratio analogue of D2 is not immediate. Resolving this is the most concrete attack on the convex-hull restriction.

    §9.3 MAH-DPO and non-scalarised multi-reward DPO#

    MAH-DPO (arXiv 2510.01167, Oct 2025). Per FC-13, author chain unverified. Attaches separate output heads to the policy network, one per objective; each head produces a per-objective log-ratio \(\log(\pi_{\theta,i}(a\mid s)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s))\), and a vector preference loss $\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAH-DPO}}\;=\;-\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a^+,a^-)}\!\Bigl[\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\sigma\!\bigl(\beta(\mathbf{r}_\theta(s,a^+)-\mathbf{r}_\theta(s,a^-))\bigr)\Bigr]\)$ or a non-scalarised analogue using a vector Bradley–Terry comparison. The architectural commitment — vector output heads rather than scalar — sidesteps the linear-scalarisation collapse of MODPO inside the loss, although the training-time weight \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) still appears as a linear combination outside the sigmoid.

    §9.4 Robust online multi-reward DPO#

    Robust MO-Online DPO (arXiv 2503.00295, Mar 2025). Per FC-13, author chain unverified. Online multi-reward DPO with an adaptive \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\): at each iteration, sample preference pairs against an ensemble of reward models and update \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) via robust min-max against the ensemble's reward-model distribution shift.

    The construct adapts the user-side preference \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) to be algorithmic in the sense of Thread B — bringing multi-reward DPO closer to the gradient-MTL community's Nash-MTL formulation noted in the B↔C stitch of §7.5.

    §9.5 Slot-2 taxonomy and the A↔C stitch#

    Slot 2 of Thread C ("multi-reward DPO") is split into three architectural choices: (i) linear-scalarised inside the log-ratio (MODPO, restatement 4 of the identity); (ii) vector output heads (MAH-DPO, non-scalarised); (iii) adaptive \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) (Robust MO-Online DPO).

    The convex-hull restriction binds (i) and the training-time-linear component of (iii); (ii) escapes the restriction architecturally but pays for it in inference-time decoding complexity (selecting which head to listen to is itself a multi-objective decision).

    The A↔C stitch that closes this section: Tchebycheff scalarisation (P3 of §2) is the canonical resolution to MODPO's convex-hull restriction but lacks a clean DPO closed form (D5); the smooth-Tchebycheff surrogate (Lin et al. ICML 2024) is the concrete attack angle; MaxMin-RLHF via Sion (X1 of §12 below) provides a partial existence proof that some non-linear-scalarised alignment is tractable.

    §9 frames the convex-hull-restriction problem; §12 exhibits one tractable non-linear-scalarised solution (MaxMin-RLHF via Sion duality); seam 1 (Tchebycheff-DPO closed form) is the substantial open question between them.

    §10 Constrained RLHF: Safe RLHF, optimal dualization, and the dual-as-scalarisation-weight identity#

    Model basis. Helpfulness reward \(R_\phi(s,a)\) and cost (harmlessness) model \(C_\psi(s,a)\) on the same prompt-response space; budget \(b\in\mathbb{R}\); Lagrange multiplier \(\eta\ge 0\) (single constraint) or \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^m_{\ge 0}\) (multi-constraint). KL temperature \(\beta>0\) and reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) as in §8–§9. The constrained-RLHF problem is $\(\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{s,\,a\sim\pi_\theta}\![R_\phi(s,a)]\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb{E}_s[\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}_{s,\,a\sim\pi_\theta}\![C_\psi(s,a)]\;\le\;b.\)$ Two algorithm families address this objective: iterative primal-dual (Safe RLHF), and one-shot dualisation with closed-form inner policy (Huang et al. CAN / MOCAN / PECAN).

    §10.1 Safe RLHF — Josef Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, Yang ICLR 2024#

    Construction. Separate reward and cost models are trained from pairwise preference data on a "is more helpful?" axis and "is less harmful?" axis respectively. The constrained objective is converted to its Lagrangian: $\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{Safe}}(\theta,\eta)\;=\;-\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[R_\phi]\,+\,\eta\,\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[C_\psi]-b\bigr)\,+\,\beta\,\mathbb{E}_s[\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]],\)$ and solved by PPO-Lagrangian iteration: at each outer step, run PPO on the scalarised reward \(R_\phi-\eta C_\psi\) for a fixed inner horizon, then update \(\eta\leftarrow[\eta+\rho_\eta(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[C_\psi]-b)]_+\). The multiplier \(\eta\) is updated by projected gradient ascent on the dual; the inner PPO solves the primal at the current \(\eta\).

    KKT identity. At convergence \((\theta^\star,\eta^\star)\), the four-condition KKT system holds: stationarity in \(\theta\) (the PPO-policy-gradient zero); complementary slackness \(\eta^\star\,(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^\star}[C_\psi]-b)=0\); primal feasibility \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^\star}[C_\psi]\le b\); dual feasibility \(\eta^\star\ge 0\).

    Complementary slackness has the structural reading: either the constraint is slack (\(\mathbb{E}[C_\psi]<b\) and \(\eta^\star=0\), helpfulness-only solution) or the constraint is binding (\(\mathbb{E}[C_\psi]=b\), harmlessness budget exhausted at the optimum). The latter is the operational regime of interest — the constraint boundary — where \(\eta^\star>0\) has a precise interpretation as an optimal scalarisation weight (§10.3 below).

    Authorship note (FC-12). Some bibliographic exports list the first author as "Juntao Dai"; the ICLR 2024 proceedings file uses "Josef Dai" (same author, Tsinghua / PKU). We use "Josef Dai" per the ICLR record throughout.

    §10.2 One-shot optimal dualisation — Huang, Li, Dobriban, Bastani, Hassani, Ding NeurIPS 2024 (spotlight)#

    Construction (CAN, MOCAN, PECAN). Huang et al. exploit the closed-form inner optimum of the KL-regularised Lagrangian to pre-optimise \(\eta\) before a single unconstrained policy update — eliminating the primal-dual iteration of Safe RLHF entirely.

    Theorem S1 (KL-Lagrangian closed-form inner optimum, Huang et al. NeurIPS 2024 Lemma; formerly T6 in §10). For the per-state Lagrangian \(\max_\pi\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[R_\phi-\eta C_\psi]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) at fixed \(\eta\ge 0\), the inner optimum is the D1 closed form with effective reward \(R_\phi-\eta C_\psi\): $\(\pi^\star_\eta(a\mid s)\;\propto\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)\,\exp\!\bigl((R_\phi(s,a)-\eta C_\psi(s,a))/\beta\bigr).\)$ The outer dual function $\(D(\eta)\;=\;\beta\,\mathbb{E}_s[\log Z_\eta(s)]\,+\,\eta\,b\,-\,\beta\,\mathbb{E}_s[\log Z_0(s)]\)$ (the value of the Lagrangian at the inner optimum, normalised) is \((G/\beta)\)-smooth (Lipschitz gradient with modulus \(G/\beta\), where \(G\) bounds the cost-model norm \(\|C_\psi\|_\infty\)) and locally strongly convex at the optimal dual \(\eta^\star\).

    The \((G/\beta)\) qualifier — FC-4. Phase 0b's original wording for S1 had this as "smooth + locally strongly convex" without the explicit modulus; the precise statement is \((G/\beta)\)-smooth (gradient Lipschitz constant \(G/\beta\), not unconditioned) and locally strongly convex (in a neighbourhood of \(\eta^\star\), not globally).

    The local qualifier is load-bearing: a converse statement of global strong convexity is not supported by the paper. v3's S10 prose adheres to this; readers should treat the convergence rate as \(O(1/T)\) in the local regime and not extrapolate to global guarantees in non-smooth or multi-constraint settings (the latter is part of seam 3 of §13).

    MOCAN and PECAN extensions. MOCAN extends the one-shot dualisation to a vector of cost constraints \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^m_{\ge 0}\), dualising each separately under the same closed-form inner step. PECAN makes the cost constraint preference-based: instead of a fixed budget \(b\) from a calibration dataset, the constraint is implicitly defined by pairwise preferences "\(a^+\) is more compliant than \(a^-\)" with \(C_\psi\) implicitly trained from the preference data.

    The structural significance. Safe RLHF iterates primal-dual; CAN does the dual once, the primal once, and is done. The computational saving is roughly an order of magnitude in compute.

    The theoretical commitment is the local strong convexity of \(D(\eta)\) at \(\eta^\star\) — without it, one-shot dualisation has no convergence guarantee, and the dual must be re-optimised iteratively. The KL regularisation is essential for both the closed-form inner optimum (S1) and the local smoothness of \(D\) — both fail without it.

    §10.3 The dual-as-scalarisation-weight identity#

    The single most structurally important fact of §10 is the identity $\(\eta^\star\quad\equiv\quad\text{optimal scalarisation weight on the constraint at the constraint boundary}.\)$ Concretely: at the constraint boundary \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^\star}[C_\psi]=b\), the Lagrangian inner optimum \(\pi^\star_{\eta^\star}\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp((R_\phi-\eta^\star C_\psi)/\beta)\) has the same form as a linear MODPO with reward vector \((R_\phi,-C_\psi)\) and preference weight \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(1,\eta^\star)/(1+\eta^\star)\in\Delta^1\) — i.e., the dual variable on the constraint, after normalisation, is the user-side preference weight that produces the same policy.

    The constrained-RLHF reformulation (slot 4 of Thread C) and the multi-reward DPO formulation (slot 2) are dual views of the same construct, with \(\eta\) on the constraint side and \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) on the objective side.

    Two structural consequences:

    (a) MOCAN with linear cost vector inherits the MODPO convex-hull restriction. If the multi-cost MOCAN problem dualises to a vector \(\boldsymbol{\eta}^\star\) and the resulting inner policy is the linear MODPO closed form \(\pi^\star\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{r}/\beta)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) proportional to the augmented \(\boldsymbol{\eta}^\star\) vector, then MOCAN's recoverable front coverage is exactly the convex hull of the alignment Pareto front — restatement (4) of the four-restatement identity again, this time on the constrained-RLHF side.

    (b) The constraint formulation is more interpretable but mathematically isomorphic to the scalarised formulation. Practitioners often argue for the constraint formulation on interpretability grounds — "the harmlessness budget is more easily set than the helpfulness/harmlessness weight." The mathematics of S1 + the dual-as-weight identity says these are equivalent at the boundary; the user-facing parameterisation differs but the policy is the same.

    (c) The identity is the A↔C stitch carried into the constrained regime. §3's KKT system (K1 of §3) generates \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) on the objective side; §10's KL-Lagrangian generates \(\eta\ge 0\) on the constraint side; the two are the same simplex multiplier up to the constraint-budget reparameterisation.

    More precisely, the four-restatement identity carries one more step: linear MODPO ≡ Safe RLHF at the constraint boundary ≡ MOCAN with linear cost ≡ Geoffrion 1968 convex-hull restriction.

    §11 Preference-conditioned single-model alignment and the LMC question#

    Model basis. Shared SFT-rooted initialisation \(\theta_0\in\mathbb{R}^p\); \(k\) per-objective fine-tunes \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k\), each obtained by RLHF / DPO on objective \(r_i\) starting from \(\theta_0\). Parameter-space simplex \(\Delta^{k-1}\) of weights \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k)\). Interpolated parameter $\(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i\,\theta_i,\qquad \boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1},\)$ and the corresponding deployed policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(a\mid s)\). The community's central question — and the only Phase 0b unifying thread (U5) that is genuinely new to the LLM era — is whether the parameter-space simplex \(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\) produces a policy whose per-objective evaluation \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}}[r_i]\) recovers (up to controllable error) the Pareto front the user would get from per-\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) retraining.

    §11.1 Linear mode connectivity — the hypothesis#

    Definition (LMC at \(k\ge 3\)). Linear mode connectivity (LMC) is the hypothesis that the \(k\) fine-tuned solutions \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k\) lie inside a single connected low-loss basin in the loss landscape such that the per-objective evaluation along the simplex interpolation is bounded by the worst endpoint plus a controllable error: $\(\forall\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1},\quad J_i(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\;\le\;J_i(\theta_i)\,+\,\varepsilon_i\qquad(i=1,\ldots,k),\)$ where \(J_i\) is the per-objective alignment evaluation and \(\varepsilon_i\ge 0\) is a uniform tolerance.

    Theorem L1 (LMC hypothesis status — open seam 2; formerly labelled T8 in §11). LMC is empirically demonstrated for \(k=2\) in pre-LLM computer-vision and language-modelling settings (Frankle et al. Linear Mode Connectivity and the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, ICLR 2020 [exact track unverified — FC-10]; Ainsworth et al. Git Re-Basin, ICLR 2023 [exact track unverified — FC-11]).

    For alignment rewards at \(k\ge 3\), no formal theorem and only limited empirical evidence is available. The Rewarded Soups and Panacea papers below operate as if LMC holds at \(k\ge 3\); the survey carries the hypothesis explicitly and flags the gap.

    Stake of L1. If LMC fails at \(k\ge 3\) alignment rewards, Rewarded Soups and Panacea cannot recover the full alignment Pareto front via parameter-space interpolation, and the entire U5 thread degrades to per-\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) retraining — exactly the cost the parameter-space programme was designed to avoid. The empirical resolution of L1 is the largest single open question in 2024–2026 alignment-MOO.

    §11.2 Rewarded Soups — Ramé, Couairon, Shukor, Dancette, Gaya, Soulier, Cord NeurIPS 2023#

    Construction. Fine-tune \(k\) checkpoints \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k\) on \(k\) proxy rewards from the same SFT initialisation \(\theta_0\); at inference, the user supplies \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) and the deployed model is \(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\sum_i\lambda_i\theta_i\).

    The Pareto-front recovery argument is empirical: if LMC holds at \(k\ge 3\), the deployed \(\theta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\) realises the convex combination of per-objective rewards (and possibly more, if the loss landscape is favourable).

    The post-hoc nature. Rewarded Soups does not know \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) at training time — the \(k\) fine-tunes are independent — so the simplex interpolation at inference is a post-hoc construction. This is a feature (one set of \(k\) fine-tunes serves all deployment preferences) and a limitation (the fine-tunes are not preference-conditioned during training, so the resulting interpolated policy may not be Pareto-optimal even if LMC holds).

    §11.3 Panacea — Zhong, Ma, Zhang, Yang, Chen, Zhang, Qi, Yang NeurIPS 2024 (preference-conditioned SVD-LoRA)#

    Construction. Panacea differs from Rewarded Soups in two crucial ways.

    First, the preference \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) is injected at training time as a multiplicative modulation of the singular values inside SVD-decomposed LoRA layers. Concretely, for a LoRA decomposition \(W=W_0+UDV^\top\) with diagonal \(D=\mathrm{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_r)\), Panacea replaces \(D\) by \(D_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\mathrm{diag}(\phi_1(\boldsymbol{\lambda})d_1,\ldots,\phi_r(\boldsymbol{\lambda})d_r)\) with \(\phi_j:\Delta^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}\) learned multiplicative gates.

    Second, a single fine-tuning run trains the LoRA factors + the gates jointly across the simplex of \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), recovering — under the LMC-like assumption — a learned preference-conditional manifold rather than a post-hoc interpolation.

    The architectural commitment. Panacea trades implementation complexity for expressiveness. The LoRA SVD modulation is more flexible than a linear interpolation of two checkpoints; the preference-conditioned training schedule means the model sees the entire simplex during fine-tuning rather than just \(k\) vertex points; and the resulting deployment is a single model parameterised by \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) at inference.

    The trade-off is that Panacea's correctness depends more heavily on the conditioning architecture being able to learn the LMC manifold — a stronger assumption than Rewarded Soups' "the manifold already exists, we just interpolate."

    Venue note. Panacea is published at NeurIPS 2024 per the proceedings poster page; the arXiv landing page (2402.02030) does not itself state the venue, and we cite both.

    §11.4 The slot-3 taxonomy and what U5 actually contributes#

    Slot 3 of Thread C ("weight-interpolation single-model alignment") is the only slot in the v3 taxonomy that lives entirely in parameter space rather than in policy / reward / constraint space.

    Its operational appeal is concrete: a single trained model serves all deployment preferences via parameter interpolation, instead of \(k!\) retrainings on every \((\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(t)})\) schedule a deployment cycles through. Its theoretical fragility is the LMC hypothesis at \(k\ge 3\) — if the basin geometry fails to extend from \(k=2\) to higher \(k\), the entire programme collapses.

    The single most plausible direction in which L1 fails — and a direction worth flagging for empirical study — is when the \(k\) objectives are internally conflicting in a way that has no \(k=2\) analogue. Helpfulness vs. harmlessness (\(k=2\)) is well-studied and LMC seems robust.

    Helpfulness vs. harmlessness vs. brevity vs. truthfulness vs. (jurisdiction-specific) safety (\(k=5\)) introduces high-dimensional simplex geometry on the loss landscape; the empirical evidence on whether LMC holds in this regime is sparse, and a single negative finding would re-route a substantial fraction of the alignment-engineering literature.

    Bilevel PEFT footnote. A related but distinct thread treats the parameter-space MOO via bilevel formulations: the lower level fine-tunes per-objective, the upper level meta-initialises. BiLoRA (venue claimed ICLR 2024 by secondary sources but not verified — FC-9; cited here as "BiLoRA (venue unverified)") and BRIDGE (arXiv 2509.06948, FC-13) are representative.

    The bilevel formulation handles non-unique lower-level minima via the lower-level singleton (LLS) relaxation classical in MOO theory; we mention these in passing because their full treatment would extend §11 beyond its budget.

    §12 Pluralistic alignment, agentic MOO, and the four-restatement thesis closure#

    Model basis. As in §9–§10, vector reward \(\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^k\), KL temperature \(\beta>0\), reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

    Additionally, for pluralistic alignment, a population of users (or population of value systems) indexed by \(i=1,\ldots,k\); per-user reward \(r_i\); saddle-point objective \(\max_\theta\min_i\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[r_i]\) (Egalitarian / Tchebycheff on the worst-coordinate).

    For agentic MOO, a discrete configuration space \(\mathcal{C}\) over tool selection / team composition; multi-objective Bayesian optimisation surrogates with hypervolume improvement as the acquisition function. For evaluation, the multi-axis HELM-style score space \(\mathbb{R}^7\) (accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, efficiency) with per-axis normalised scores.

    §12.1 MaxMin-RLHF as Tchebycheff-on-the-worst-coordinate#

    MaxMin-RLHF — Chakraborty, Qiu, Yuan, Koppel, Huang, Manocha, Bedi, Wang ICML 2024 (arXiv 2402.08925). Per FC-8, the author list is the 8-author roster on the arXiv page; Phase 0c Thread C carried a spurious 9-author version with "Ramesh" and "Jain" and missing "Alec Koppel" — that error is corrected in this registry and must not propagate.

    The ICML 2024 venue is per icml.cc/virtual/2024/poster/34828; the arXiv landing page does not itself state the venue (parallel to the FC-3 IPO hedge). MaxMin-RLHF drops the single-reward-model assumption: a mixture of reward models (one per user group) is learned via EM on preference data, and the policy is aligned against the worst-group reward via the Egalitarian-principle objective $\(\max_\theta\,\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}}\,\mathbb{E}_{s,\,a\sim\pi_\theta}[r_i(s,a)].\)$

    Theorem X1 (MaxMin via Sion duality — partial existence proof for non-linear-scalarised DPO; formerly T9 in §12). The MaxMin objective above admits the saddle-point reformulation $\(\max_\theta\,\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}}\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbb{E}_{s,\,a\sim\pi_\theta}[\mathbf{r}(s,a)].\)$ Under convex-concave compactness conditions of Sion's minimax theorem (convex in \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), concave in \(\theta\) when the relevant set is compact and convex), the order of max and min can be swapped: $\(\max_\theta\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\mathbf{r}]\;=\;\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\max_\theta\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\mathbf{r}].\)$ The inner \(\max_\theta\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\mathbf{r}]\) at fixed \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is exactly the D4 (linear MODPO) inner optimum, which admits the closed form \(\pi^\star_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{r}/\beta)\). The outer \(\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\) on the simplex is a finite-dimensional convex optimisation.

    MaxMin-RLHF is therefore implementable via alternating closed-form policy updates (inner \(\max\)) and projected gradient descent on \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) (outer \(\min\)).

    The structural significance. X1 is the partial existence proof for non-linear-scalarised DPO noted as open seam 1 of §13. The pointwise Tchebycheff scalarisation \(g_{\mathrm{TC}}=\max_i\lambda_i(r_i-z_i^\star)\) has no clean DPO closed form (D5); the MaxMin objective, which is mathematically the worst-coordinate Tchebycheff when \(\mathbf{z}^\star=\mathbf{0}\), does admit a closed-form inner-policy step via the Sion-dual reformulation.

    MaxMin-RLHF is therefore the first tractable non-linear-scalarised alignment objective that escapes the convex-hull restriction of linear MODPO. It is restatement (4) of the four-restatement identity in its escape mode: where MODPO is bounded by Geoffrion 1968, MaxMin-RLHF reaches the non-convex portion of the Pareto front via the Sion-dual route.

    The Sion convex-concave caveat. Sion's theorem requires: (a) the objective convex+lsc in the inner-min variable, concave+usc in the outer-max variable; (b) compactness on at least one side.

    The objective \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\mathbf{r}]\) is linear in \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) on the compact simplex \(\Delta^{k-1}\) (linearity implies both convexity and concavity in \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\)), so the inner-min side is licensed. The \(\theta\)-concavity requirement is conditional: it holds in expectation if the policy class is soft-max on fixed features, but is a modelling assumption for general neural-network policies.

    Compactness on the bounded side is carried by the value-vector image \(\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\mathbf{r}]\}\subseteq[-R_{\max},R_{\max}]^k\) (immediate from bounded reward models), not by \(\theta\in\mathbb{R}^p\) itself (which is unbounded for transformers). v3 cites Sion's theorem with these hypotheses explicit, per FC-6, and treats X1 as a conditional existence proof — sufficient to motivate seam 1 attacks, not sufficient to claim a closed-form Tchebycheff-DPO in full generality.

    §12.2 The Roadmap to Pluralistic Alignment — Sorensen, Moore, Fisher, Gordon, Mireshghallah, Rytting, Ye, Jiang, Lu, Dziri, Althoff, Choi ICML 2024 position paper#

    Three operationalisations of pluralism. The Sorensen et al. position paper (ICML 2024, arXiv 2402.05070) identifies three operational forms of pluralism that distinguish what an aligned model is supposed to do under value disagreement:

    (i) Overton-pluralistic — produce a spectrum of reasonable responses spanning the Overton window of legitimate views; (ii) steerably-pluralistic — be steerable to specific perspectives at inference time; (iii) distributionally-pluralistic — produce responses whose distribution is calibrated to the population's value distribution.

    The paper's argument against averaging diverse user preferences into a scalar utility is the conceptual motivation for vector-reward MORL (§7) and MaxMin-RLHF (§12.1). The argument is position rather than theorem: Sorensen et al. do not claim MaxMin-RLHF is optimal in all three pluralism modes, only that scalar averaging fails all three.

    The taxonomy is useful for the v3 reader because it makes the user-side question — "what does pluralism mean operationally?" — distinct from the algorithm-side question — "what is the MOO formalisation?".

    §12.3 Agentic MOO and bilevel PEFT — MALBO, BRIDGE#

    MALBO (arXiv 2511.11788, Nov 2025). Per FC-13, author chain unverified. Treats agentic workflow design (team composition, tool selection, prompt routing) as a multi-objective Bayesian optimisation problem over a discrete configuration space \(\mathcal{C}\). Cost-vs-accuracy Pareto fronts are tracked via expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI) as the acquisition function, with the hypervolume indicator \(\mathcal{H}\) from H1/H2 of §4.

    MALBO lives outside the differentiable-policy regime of §8–§11 — there is no gradient through the agentic configuration — and is consequently disconnected from the gradient-MOO machinery of Thread B. The lack of a differentiable bridge is open seam 4 of §13.

    BRIDGE (arXiv 2509.06948, Sep 2025). Per FC-13, author chain unverified. Casts the SFT-then-RL fine-tuning pipeline as a bilevel MOO problem: the lower level fine-tunes each task-specific model from a shared meta-initialisation; the upper level optimises the meta-initialisation against a multi-objective alignment criterion.

    The lower-level singleton (LLS) relaxation handles the non-uniqueness of lower-level minima — a classical MOO loosening trick that lifts cleanly to the alignment regime. BRIDGE bridges classical MOO (bilevel formulation) with PEFT (LoRA-based per-task fine-tuning).

    §12.4 The Pareto-aware benchmark gap — HELM, HELM-Safety, BBEH, MT-Bench, LiveBench#

    Theorem X2 (Pareto-aware benchmark gap, operational claim — open seam 5; formerly T10 in §12). Across HELM v1 (Liang et al. 2022–2023+), HELM-Safety (Stanford CRFM, November 2024), BIG-Bench, BIG-Bench Extra Hard (Kazemi et al. arXiv 2502.19187, 2025), MT-Bench, LiveBench, and AIR-Bench 2024: multi-axis scores are displayed but aggregated by arithmetic mean over normalised benchmark scores and scalar-ranked. No public 2024–2026 LLM benchmark computes the non-dominated set of model variants in the multi-axis score space and reports the corresponding Pareto front.

    The operational character of the claim (FC-7). X2 is not a theoretical result; it is an empirical claim about benchmark reporting conventions. The single most explicit grounding is Stanford CRFM's HELM-Safety release note (crfm.stanford.edu/2024/11/08/helm-safety.html), which describes the aggregation rule verbatim as "the mean over all benchmark scores normalised to (0,1)."

    Kazemi et al.'s BBEH paper reports per-task means; no Pareto front. The other benchmarks listed share this convention.

    Why the gap matters. The four-restatement identity culminates in the measurement layer: a community that reports its model evaluations as scalar means is institutionally unable to observe the Pareto front of model variants, even when the MOO theory in §1–§11 says that front is the right object to study.

    The gap is operational (a reporting convention) rather than theoretical (no benchmark organisation has yet swapped scalar rank for a Pareto-frontier display) and is therefore resolvable with a single benchmark publication. Open seam 5 of §13 sketches the attack angle.

    §12.5 The four-restatement closure#

    The survey's signature thesis, repeated at §1, lives at this point as a closed argument:

    1. OR/MCDM (Theorem P2 of §2, Geoffrion 1968). Linear scalarisation \(g_{\mathrm{LS}}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^\top\mathbf{f}\) is complete only under convexity; in the non-convex case, sweeping \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) recovers only the convex hull of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\).
    2. Gradient MTL (Theorem M6 of §5, Liu et al. NeurIPS 2021; FC-5). Only CAGrad guarantees stationarity of the average loss; MGDA, MGDA-UB, PCGrad, and Nash-MTL admit Pareto-stationary points that are not Pareto-optimal under non-convex per-task losses. The gap is restatement (1) in gradient space.
    3. MORL (Theorem V2 of §7, Roijers JAIR 2013 / Hayes JAAMAS 2022). \(\mathrm{CCS}\subseteq\mathrm{PCS}\); strict containment under non-linear utility. A linear-utility-trained MORL agent visits only \(\mathrm{CCS}\). The gap is restatement (1) in MORL value-vector space.
    4. RLHF (Theorem D4 of §9, Zhou et al. ACL Findings 2024; the constrained-RLHF MOCAN-linear extension of §10). Linear MODPO inherits the convex-hull restriction; non-linear MaxMin-RLHF (X1 of §12, Chakraborty et al. ICML 2024) escapes it via Sion duality. The gap is restatement (1) in alignment policy space.

    Four restatements of one theorem across four communities — and the theorem is Geoffrion 1968. The escape from each restatement (Tchebycheff P3 in OR/MCDM; CAGrad in gradient MTL; non-linear utility envelope methods in MORL; MaxMin-RLHF / smooth-Tchebycheff-DPO in RLHF) is the same escape: trade the speed of linear scalarisation for the completeness of non-linear scalarisation.

    This is the closing payoff of v3. The five open seams of §13 below each address one face of this identity: seam 1 (Tchebycheff-DPO closed form) addresses the RLHF face; seam 2 (LMC at \(k\ge 3\)) addresses the parameter-space face; seam 3 (global convergence of one-shot dualisation) addresses the constrained-RLHF face; seam 4 (differentiable agentic MOO) addresses the agentic / discrete face; seam 5 (Pareto-aware benchmarks) addresses the measurement face.

    §12.6 Cross-domain anchor pool — Sen, Markowitz, Thomson#

    The five-community map of this survey can be sharpened by three classical non-AI anchors from welfare economics, mathematical finance, and cooperative game theory. Each anchor is independent of the LLM era and provides a transferable proof technique (or a transferable cautionary tale).

    Deep-diver P1 surfaces them as zero-cost augmentations to the four-restatement identity; we record them here so v4 can develop them further if the literature warrants.

    Anchor 1 — Sen 1970 (Collective Choice and Social Welfare; reissued Penguin 2017). Amartya Sen's framework formalises the aggregation of individual preferences into a social welfare function \(W:\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}\) (one coordinate per agent / objective) and proves a family of impossibility / restriction theorems on how \(W\) can simultaneously satisfy Pareto-efficiency, anonymity, monotonicity, and independence of irrelevant alternatives.

    The transfer. HELM-axis aggregation (the gap of X2 / §12.4) is structurally a Sen-style aggregation problem: the seven HELM axes are seven "agents" in welfare-economic language, and the arithmetic-mean aggregation \(W_{\mathrm{HELM}}=k^{-1}\sum_i s_i\) is the utilitarian SWF. Sen 1970 (and the subsequent Sen 1976, Econometrica) catalogues the failure modes of utilitarian aggregation under heterogeneous preferences — the same failure modes that make HELM arithmetic-mean rankings Pareto-unaware.

    More precisely, the Pareto-aware-benchmark seam 5 sharpens once it is reframed in Sen's vocabulary: HELM is not just "missing a Pareto front"; it is committed to utilitarian SWF as the social-choice axiom, and an Egalitarian SWF \(W_{\mathrm{Egal}}=\min_i s_i\) (the X1 / MaxMin restatement) is a documented Sen-alternative that maps to the worst-coordinate Tchebycheff scalarisation.

    Anchor 2 — Markowitz 1952 (Portfolio Selection, Journal of Finance 7(1):77–91, March 1952). Harry Markowitz's mean-variance portfolio frontier is the efficient frontier of expected return \(\mu\) vs. variance \(\sigma^2\) over portfolio weights \(\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{n-1}\) (long-only) or \(\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^n\) (long-short).

    The transfer. The efficient frontier is literally the Pareto front of the bi-objective problem \(\min_{\mathbf{w}}(-\mu(\mathbf{w}),\sigma^2(\mathbf{w}))\) in the convex case (covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) positive semi-definite makes \(\sigma^2\) convex; expected return \(\mu\) is linear). Markowitz's two-fund theorem and capital-market-line construction are exactly P2-style results — linear scalarisation \(g_{\mathrm{LS}}=-\mu+\gamma\sigma^2\) recovers the convex Pareto front by sweeping the risk-aversion coefficient \(\gamma>0\).

    The cautionary tale. When the objective is not mean-variance — e.g. Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), Rockafellar–Uryasev 2000 — the efficient frontier becomes non-convex in \((\mu,\mathrm{CVaR})\) space and linear scalarisation fails (Pflug 2000 / Mansini–Ogryczak–Speranza 2014). Markowitz's bi-objective convex case is the cleanest worked example of P2's hypothesis being satisfied; CVaR's non-convex case is the cleanest worked example of P2's hypothesis failing.

    The transfer to alignment: helpfulness vs. harmlessness with Bradley–Terry preferences may be convex (linear MODPO suffices); helpfulness vs. worst-tail-toxicity (CVaR-style risk measure on the toxicity model) is structurally non-convex, and only X1-style non-linear-scalarised alignment recovers the full front.

    Anchor 3 — Thomson 2016 ("Bargaining and the theory of cooperative games: John Nash and beyond", International Journal of Game Theory survey; cf. Thomson 1994, Cooperative models of bargaining in Aumann–Hart Handbook). William Thomson's survey of cooperative bargaining unifies the Nash, Kalai–Smorodinsky, and Egalitarian bargaining solutions through four axioms: (N1) Pareto-efficiency, (N2) symmetry, (N3) independence of irrelevant alternatives, (N4) scale-invariance / individual-rationality.

    Nash 1950 is the unique solution satisfying N1+N2+N3+N4; Kalai–Smorodinsky 1975 swaps N3 for monotonicity; the Egalitarian solution swaps N3 for the Egalitarian-equity axiom.

    The transfer. Nash-MTL of §5.4 (M7) is a direct lift of Nash's bargaining solution to the gradient-MTL setting: each task is a player, per-task descent rate is utility, the Nash product \(\prod_i u_i\) is maximised over the strategy space.

    The cautionary tale (deep-diver P1). Thomson's survey is explicit that N2 (symmetry) and N3 (IIA) both fail in many practical bargaining problems — and these failures are inherited by Nash-MTL when lifted to LLM alignment.

    Specifically: (i) N2 fails when objectives have different scales (helpfulness on a 0–1 Bradley–Terry probability vs. toxicity on an integer count of toxic tokens): the Nash product is not scale-invariant under arbitrary affine reparameterisations, and Nash-MTL's \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^\star\) depends on the unit choice in a way that has no principled defence.

    (ii) N3 fails when adding a third objective changes the relative weights of the first two: in alignment, adding "calibration" as a third axis can flip which of "helpfulness" / "harmlessness" gets the larger Nash weight relative to its two-axis configuration.

    The N2/N3 failures of cooperative bargaining are the non-LLM ancestor of the Nash-MTL instability that Liu et al. NeurIPS 2021 (CAGrad) documents empirically. Treating Nash-MTL as "the principled MOO method" without auditing the bargaining-axiom transfer is a category error.

    Aggregate use of the anchor pool. Sen 1970 sharpens seam 5 (Pareto-aware benchmarks \(\equiv\) utilitarian-vs-Egalitarian SWF choice). Markowitz 1952 supplies the convex worked example for P2 and the CVaR cautionary tale for non-convex alignment objectives. Thomson 2016 explains why Nash-MTL is fragile under heterogeneous-scale alignment objectives — N2/N3 transfer-failure, not a neural-network artefact.

    None of these anchors is LLM-era; all three are zero-cost in the sense that they exist in cited form without further verification cost. v4 should consider whether one or more deserves a dedicated cross-domain section.

    §13 Open seams and outlook (closing prose)#

    Seam 1 — Tchebycheff-DPO / achievement-DPO closed form. The cleanest open question in Thread C. The smooth-Tchebycheff surrogate \(g_{\mathrm{ST}}^{(\mu)}\) of Lin et al. (ICML 2024, arXiv 2402.19078) plugs into D1 of §8 and yields a well-defined candidate policy \(\pi^\star\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(g_{\mathrm{ST}}^{(\mu)}/\beta)\).

    The remaining theoretical question is whether the Bradley–Terry implicit-reward parameterisation of D2 (the partition \(Z(s)\) cancellation) survives the smooth-max — i.e. the separability obstacle of §9.2 (additive-separable rewards yield factorisable exponentials and the \(Z(s)\) cancellation; \(\max\) destroys separability). X1 of §12 shows MaxMin-RLHF already escapes linear scalarisation via Sion duality — partial existence proof. A clean smooth-Tchebycheff-DPO would unify the two routes.

    Seam 2 — Linear mode connectivity at \(k\ge 3\) alignment rewards. The most consequential empirical question in U5. A negative finding (LMC fails at \(k\ge 3\)) would not invalidate Panacea — its architectural SVD modulation may impose a learned LMC that post-hoc Rewarded Soups cannot enforce — but would reroute the field.

    Attack angles: (i) systematic measurement of basin connectivity along \(\Delta^{k-1}\) for \(k=3,4,5\) alignment rewards, with loss-barrier scaling in \(k\); (ii) theoretical extension of Ainsworth-style permutation symmetries from pairs to \(k\)-tuples, where the combinatorial blow-up of symmetric-group permutations on hidden units is the obstacle.

    Seam 3 — Global convergence of one-shot dualisation for non-smooth or multi-constraint costs. S1 of §10 gives \((G/\beta)\)-smooth + locally strongly convex at \(\eta^\star\), but global convergence rates and the \(m>1\)-constraint extension are not established in the published paper.

    Replacing the smooth cost model \(C_\psi\) by a sum of indicator constraints (per-token toxicity gates, say) makes the dual piecewise-smooth; generalised-gradient analysis via Clarke subdifferentials is the relevant tool. For \(m>1\) constraints, MOCAN's vector \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) formulation should remain convex but the local-strong-convexity radius may shrink with \(m\).

    Seam 4 — Differentiable agentic MOO. MALBO (C14 of §12.3) is discrete BO; no gradient MOO formulation for agentic LLM workflows currently exists.

    Attack angles: (i) Gumbel-softmax relaxation of tool / agent selection followed by MGDA min-norm (Thread B §5) on the continuous surrogate; the discrete–continuous gap appears in the relaxation temperature schedule. (ii) REINFORCE-with-baseline for the discrete combinatorial choice combined with MODPO-style scalarisation of cost vs. accuracy — bridges discrete BO and gradient MOO through the score-function estimator.

    Seam 5 — Pareto-front-aware LLM benchmarks. X2 of §12 documents the gap. Attack angle: compute the non-dominated set of model variants in the seven-dimensional HELM-axis space, report the Pareto front + hypervolume indicator \(\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A};\mathbf{r})\) from §4. The obstacle is institutional — a reporting-convention shift rather than a theoretical advance. A single benchmark paper that swaps scalar rank for Pareto frontier display would re-anchor the field.

    Closing — v3 → v4 reproducibility note#

    The four-restatement closure of §12.5 is the survey's signature payoff; the five open seams above (1–5) are the operational research agenda. We do not repeat the four-restatement enumeration here — it is the load-bearing closure of §12.5 and re-stating it would be redundant. Instead we record the two practical takeaways that any implementer of §5–§12 needs to internalise, and one v3 → v4 reproducibility commitment that situates the closing.

    Practical takeaway 1 (simplex multiplier). The symbol \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) (user-side preference), the symbol \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) (algorithm-side mixing weight in MGDA/PCGrad/CAGrad/Nash-MTL), and the symbol \(\eta\) (constraint dual at the constraint boundary in Safe RLHF / CAN / MOCAN / PECAN) are three names for the same simplex multiplier in the KKT system of §3 (K1), observed by three communities under three operational interpretations.

    The cross-community taxonomy of §3.2 makes this explicit; readers porting an algorithm between communities should expect to translate names rather than reformulate objects.

    Practical takeaway 2 (KL direction). The reverse KL direction \(\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) in the D1 / D2 closed form of §8 is load-bearing — swap it for the forward direction \(\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\text{ref}}\,\|\,\pi_\theta]\) and the entire closed-form machinery collapses (no soft-max optimum; mode-covering rather than mode-seeking). This direction is the single error that most reliably breaks a DPO implementation.

    v3 → v4 reproducibility commitment. v3 numbers theorems by section-prefix (P/K/H/M/V/D/S/L/X) to eliminate the v1/v2 collisions where five different theorems all carried the label "T1".

    v4 should preserve this prefixing scheme and add: (i) an open-source companion notebook implementing the D1 closed form and the M6 CAGrad descent under a single API; (ii) an extension to Tchebycheff-DPO if seam 1 closes; (iii) an extension to V2-grounded non-linear-utility envelope methods if seam 2 (LMC at \(k\ge 3\)) is empirically resolved. These commitments are not retrospective claims — they are forward markers for v4 reviewers.

    D. Cross-domain conceptual references (§12.6 anchor pool)#

    1. Sen, Collective Choice and Social Welfare, Holden-Day 1970 (Penguin reissue 2017). ★★★ The welfare-economics foundation that v3 §12.6 / §12.4 imports to reframe the Pareto-aware-benchmark gap (theorem X2, FC-7) as a Sen-style aggregation problem: HELM's arithmetic-mean aggregation is the utilitarian social welfare function \(W_{\mathrm{Util}}=k^{-1}\sum_i s_i\); the X1 / MaxMin restatement maps to Sen's Egalitarian SWF \(W_{\mathrm{Egal}}=\min_i s_i\).

    Read for the deep-structural sense of why HELM "is not just missing a Pareto front; it is committed to a social-choice axiom". Author: Amartya Sen. The 1970 monograph (plus Sen 1976 Econometrica) is the cleanest single source.

    1. Markowitz, "Portfolio Selection", Journal of Finance 7(1):77–91, March 1952. ★★★ The original derivation of the mean–variance efficient frontier — literally the Pareto front of the bi-objective problem \(\min_{\mathbf{w}}(-\mu(\mathbf{w}),\sigma^2(\mathbf{w}))\) under PSD covariance \(\Sigma\). v3 §12.6 uses this as the cleanest worked example of P2's hypothesis being satisfied (convex case, linear scalarisation \(-\mu+\gamma\sigma^2\) sweeps the front).

    Reading the 1952 paper alongside §2.2 of v3 is the fastest way to internalise why the convex-hull restriction matters: the cautionary tale — Conditional-Value-at-Risk (CVaR) under Rockafellar–Uryasev 2000 generates a non-convex frontier where linear scalarisation fails — is the financial-economics ancestor of v3's helpfulness-vs-worst-tail-toxicity argument. Author: Harry Markowitz.

    1. Thomson, "Bargaining and the theory of cooperative games: John Nash and beyond", survey chapter, Handbook of Game Theory / International Journal of Game Theory, 2016 (cf. Thomson 1994 in Aumann–Hart Handbook). ★★★ The cooperative-bargaining survey that v3 §12.6 imports to explain why Nash-MTL (§5.4) is fragile under heterogeneous-scale alignment objectives.

    The N2 (symmetry) and N3 (IIA) axioms both fail in many practical bargaining problems, and these failures are inherited by Nash-MTL when lifted to LLM alignment. Read for the deep-diver P1 derivation in §12.6 anchor 3: the N2/N3 transfer-failure is the non-LLM ancestor of the Nash-MTL instability that CAGrad (entry 11) documents empirically. Author: William Thomson.

A serious reader of §12 should consult this to see the framing v3 declines to take.

    2. Sorensen et al., Roadmap to Pluralistic Alignment, ICML 2024 (entry 21 above, recapitulated here for the contrarian slot). ★★ The Sorensen et al. roadmap argues implicitly that all three of its modes (Overton / Steerable / Distributional) are achievable via universal one-model alignment with the right preference conditioning — the position closest to Rewarded Soups (entry 15) and Panacea (entry 16).

    The contrarian reading: v3 §11 / §12 treats the LMC-at-\(k\ge 3\) hypothesis as open, with a non-trivial probability that Universal One-Model Alignment fails for non-convex preference geometries; the Sorensen / Rewarded Soups / Panacea programme implicitly assumes it succeeds. Reading these together exposes the most consequential open seam of the survey (seam 2 of §13).

    1. The implicit "linear scalarisation suffices in convex MOO" position. ★ Not a single paper but a position widely held in operations-research practice and in early-2020s MTL papers: when the Pareto front is convex, linear scalarisation by sweeping \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Delta^{k-1}\) recovers all proper Pareto optima (Geoffrion 1968, entry 4).

    v3's four-restatement thesis is the rebuttal: in LLM alignment, the assumption of convex front is unwarranted (helpfulness vs worst-tail-toxicity is the Markowitz-CVaR analogue of entry 35), and the linear-scalarisation-suffices position degrades to "MODPO covers only the convex hull of \(\mathcal{P}^\star\)". Read entries 4, 11, 17, 20, 22, and 35 together to see the v3-internal version of this debate; the position itself is the dialectical foil rather than a citeable paper.
